📖 Easing Workplace Anxiety

A lot of us want to learn how to get more done in a day. According to a Human Workplace Index survey of 3,000 full-time employees in the United States, United Kingdom and Ireland, just over half—54%—say they do feel productive at work. While this sounds positive, it comes with baggage. Nearly 83% of respondents said they also have productivity anxiety.

"Productivity anxiety is wondering, 'Am I doing enough,' despite how much you're objectively doing," says Meisha-ann Martin, Ph.D., senior director of people analytics and research for the employee recognition platform Workhuman. "It's also questioning, 'Am I working on the right things?'"

Several factors cause productivity anxiety, including companies that encourage a hustle culture, where employees are encouraged and rewarded for doing more. Today's layoff environment is also adding fuel to the fire, inserting a sense of uncertainty. And productivity anxiety also comes when there is a feedback gap issue, says Martin. "Anxiety can get worse in a space where those things are not clear."

What employees can do

If you're an employee who is feeling productivity anxiety and your manager doesn't actively address it, Martin recommends scheduling your own check-in and being direct.

"Ask targeted and specific questions like, 'Am I working on the right things?'" she says. "Be proactive by saying when you're feeling overwhelmed and asking for help prioritizing your workload."

For example, Martin suggests saying, "It feels like there's a lot on my plate. I want to make sure I'm working on the right things. What is the highest priority right now? Is there anything that I can push until later?"

What employers should do

While employees are feeling the direct impact of productivity anxiety, it also creates issues for employers, says Martin.

"When you're feeling anxious about anything, you're not your best self," she says. "If you're not feeling secure at work, it can affect how you behave, especially around innovation and risk-taking."

To help employees bring their best selves to the job, managers need to set explicit expectations, so employees know they're working on the right things.

[Managers should] clearly define performance goals with milestones and measurements, and prioritize tasks based on key business objectives. "That should take some of the anxiety away," says Martin.

Recognition also plays a part. "When people are recognized, they report lower stress," says Martin. "Recognition sends a signal that says, 'You're doing just fine. We appreciate you. It might be a tough time, but you're doing well.'"

It also helps when leaders establish regular check-in times. If you don't know that a team member is feeling anxious, you can't address it, says Martin. "I'm a big fan of a check-in that isn't just about the tasks that people do or goals and outcomes," she says. "Also check in on the person and how they're doing so that you can provide support as appropriate."

It's not uncommon for an employee to have something in their personal life impact what they're expected to do at work. Leaders should also watch for out-of-character behaviors that might indicate there is something going on.

"When a leader and employee have a great relationship, it is easier to see when things are not what they usually are," says Martin.

The cost of anxiety

Finally, managers can send signals to their team that their well-being is important. For example, they can model good restorative behavior by taking advantage of the resources that the company offers, such as paid time off, mental health support and flexible working policies.

Productivity anxiety impacts everyone. Individuals suffer in terms of their personal well-being, which bleeds into things like absenteeism, low performance and even leaving the organization. Productivity anxiety can impact organizational culture. Left to fester, it can create a culture of toxic competition, firefighting, working nights and weekends, and burnout, says Martin.

"The employee has a role, but the organization and the leader need to create that space that is psychologically safe enough for employees to talk about these things," says Martin.

By setting clear expectations and opening lines of communication, you and your team can turn productivity into a positive topic again.
