🤝 Tactics to Help You Work with Difficult People

Based on an article about workplace psychology and conflict management

Two Kinds of "Difficult"

When we run into someone we find hard to work with, our first instinct is often to shy away instead of dealing with the situation head-on. But difficult people exist everywhere, so learning to turn a tense interaction into a productive one is a skill worth building. The author splits difficult people into two very different groups. "Truly difficult" people are chronically low-energy, inward-looking, negative about everything, and unable to imagine what's possible — for this group, the advice is blunt: life is too short to keep spending your energy there. The second group, insecure people, act difficult because they are anxious and risk-averse; they see danger in almost every situation, and a few can even be downright nasty or abusive. But unlike the first group, insecurity is an opportunity — helping someone feel less insecure can unlock their potential and improve their overall wellbeing.

Reframing How You See Them

The first step in facing a difficult person is switching into "detective mode" — taking your own feelings and needs out of the equation and treating the person like an unfamiliar piece of gym equipment: intimidating at first, but genuinely useful once you learn how to use it. Another mental trick: remind yourself that this person is somebody's child, then ask whether you'd want someone else thinking about your own child the way you're currently thinking about them. That question tends to switch your empathy back on. From there, the advice is to be deliberate — plan your interactions with difficult people instead of reacting on autopilot, and treat each encounter as a chance to learn something.

Four Practical Tactics

  • Take the meeting outside, walking side by side. Face-to-face conversations force your brain to do two things at once — process what's being said and gauge the other person's facial expressions — which slows down your thinking. Walking side by side removes that second task, so both people can think more clearly during a tense conversation.
  • Schedule it after sundown. The underlying logic is that humans have poor night vision, and when we can't see well we feel more vulnerable. To reduce that feeling, people instinctively become more cooperative and less oppositional, since staying close to others feels safer than staying in conflict.
  • Offer something hot to drink. Physical temperature seems to shape social temperature: in one classic study, people who held a hot cup of coffee before reading a description of a stranger judged that person more warmly than people who had held an iced coffee.
  • Open with small talk about bad weather. Naming a negative feeling — even something as small as complaining about the rain — makes people consciously aware of that feeling, which seems to blunt its power to color their mood for the rest of the conversation.

💡 Closing Reflection

Most of these tactics aren't really about the other person at all — they're about managing the environment so both people can think and react more calmly. Before your next difficult conversation, ask yourself: what's the setting, and could a small change to it change the outcome?
