🥤🍅 Wait, Coke and Ketchup Were Once Medicine?

How food products reinvent their image over time

💬 Useful Expressions

Believe it or not, …→ Aunque no lo creas, …Believe it or not, ketchup was once sold as a remedy.
That's news to me.→ Eso es nuevo para mí / No lo sabía."Coke was a tonic?" "Yes." "Really? That's news to me."
It just goes to show that …→ Eso demuestra que …It just goes to show that marketing shapes what we believe.
Take it with a grain of salt.→ Tómalo con pinzas / con cautela.Early health claims should be taken with a grain of salt.

📖 The Article

Foods can change their image over time. Some products once believed to have healing power are now simply snacks or drinks. Two famous cases are Coca-Cola and ketchup. Today, most people think of Coca-Cola as a sugary, fizzy soda to go with fast food. Its original recipe, however, was vastly different. Dr. John S. Pemberton launched it in the late 1800s as a medicinal tonic.
Pemberton had been badly injured in the Civil War and developed a severe morphine addiction. He created a sweet syrup from coca leaves and kola nut, hoping for a pick-me-up without alcohol or other drugs. He sold it as a cure-all to be mixed into soda water, and ads claimed it eased headaches, nerve pain, and even a low mood. Back then, this sounded believable: carbonated water was thought to be healthy, and doctors often recommended coca leaves as a stimulant.
As people realized how addictive and harmful cocaine is, public concern grew, and the company took out the cocaine-containing extract. It kept using coca leaves with the cocaine removed, while caffeine provided a mild lift. Better bottling soon made fizzy drinks commonplace, and people gradually stopped seeing them mainly as health products. What began as a medicinal concoction became a refreshing indulgence enjoyed worldwide.
Ketchup has also been rebranded along the way. Most people picture a sweet, tangy dip for fries, but that was not always the case. Its roots are often traced to southern China, where coastal communities made a fermented fish sauce, sometimes called "kue-chiap." It was long-lasting and easy to store, so sailors carried it on their voyages. By the 1700s it had spread to Europe, where cooks adapted the recipe using anchovies, shallots, horseradish, and nutmeg.
Modern tomato ketchup emerged in the US in the early 1800s, when tomatoes were being promoted as a healthy food, even as a help for liver problems. One American doctor sold tomato ketchup as a remedy for indigestion. Many doctors dismissed these claims, yet its popularity kept growing. Only with the fast-food boom of the mid-1900s did ketchup gain its current image as the natural partner of fries, burgers, and hot dogs. A drink once sold as a cure and a sauce once linked to health benefits are now ordinary parts of daily meals, which shows how food, culture, and marketing keep reshaping what we eat and drink.

🗣️ Discussion Questions

  1. Why do you think people in the 1800s so readily believed that a fizzy syrup could cure headaches and low mood? Are we any less easy to persuade today?
  2. Think of a product today that is sold with health claims (a supplement, a "superfood," an energy drink). How would you decide whether those claims are credible?
  3. The article suggests that culture and marketing, not just the food itself, decide what a product "is." Do you agree? Where do you see this in your own industry?

✏️ Self-Practice

1. Use "claim" as both a noun and a verb in two sentences about a safety or HR policy.

Example: The vendor claims the new helmet passes every test, but we need proof. / That claim was never verified by our EHS team.

2. Replace "took out" with a more formal verb, then rewrite: "The company took out the harmful ingredient."

Suggested: removed, eliminated, or withdrew. "The company removed the harmful ingredient." Phrasal verbs are natural in speech; the formal verb suits reports.

3. Your manager says a new tool "cures all our problems." Respond using "Take it with a grain of salt" or "It just goes to show that …".

Example: "That sounds like a cure-all claim. I'd take it with a grain of salt until we run a pilot." Any answer that questions the claim politely and proposes evidence is a strong one.

4. Explain in two sentences how something at your company was "rebranded" or changed its image.

Model: "Our IT help desk used to be seen as a place for complaints. After a rebranding as a 'support partner,' people started to contact us earlier." Judge your answer by clarity and by whether it shows a before and after.
