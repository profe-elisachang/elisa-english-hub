🔬 Fake Studies: A Problem Science Can't Ignore

Around the world, thousands of fake scientific papers are being churned out every year — and many of them make it into respected journals. Behind much of this is a shadow industry known as a paper mill: a business that writes fake studies and sells authorship to real scientists who need publications for their résumés. These companies are parasitic — they don't add anything to science, they just feed off it. Desperate researchers, under pressure to publish, are an easy target to snag.

Vocabulary — Paragraph 1
churn out
(phr. v.) to produce large amounts quickly, usually of low quality
producir en masa
"The department churns out safety reports nobody reads."
paper mill
(n.) a company that produces fake academic papers for sale
fábrica de papers
"The journal was investigated for publishing paper mill content."
parasitic
(adj.) living off others without giving anything back
parasitario
"That vendor has a parasitic relationship with the IT budget."
snag
(v.) to get or catch something quickly
atrapar, conseguir
"HR managed to snag two great candidates before the deadline."

How are these papers made? Some are invented from whole cloth — meaning there is no real research behind them at all. Others are plagiarized: graphs, images and text lifted from real studies. With AI tools now available, paper mills can generate text that sounds far more plausible than before, making fake papers harder to spot at a glance.

Vocabulary — Paragraph 2
whole cloth
(n., idiom) complete invention, with no basis in fact
pura invención
"The incident report was made up from whole cloth."
plagiarize
(v.) to use someone else's work and present it as your own
plagiar
"He was fired for plagiarizing a colleague's training slides."
plausible
(adj.) seeming likely to be true or believable
creíble, plausible
"Give me a plausible explanation for the missing inventory."

The damage doesn't stay contained. Entire fields can be beset by fake papers, some of which get cited again and again in later research — even after they are proven false. Standards of medical care can become warped when treatments are based on studies that were never real. Journals that are laden with fabricated findings put readers — and sometimes patients — at real risk.

Vocabulary — Paragraph 3
beset
(adj.) troubled by, full of problems
acosado, plagado de
"The project was beset by delays from day one."
warped
(adj.) twisted away from what is normal or correct
deformado, distorsionado
"Constant overtime gave him a warped sense of what's normal."
laden
(adj.) carrying or full of something
cargado de
"The audit report was laden with errors."

Why does this keep happening? Experts point to a perverse incentive system: scientists are rewarded for the number of papers they publish, not the quality of the work. Hiring and grant committees often don't have the wherewithal — the time, expertise, or resources — to properly evaluate what they're reading, so they lean on raw publication counts instead.

Vocabulary — Paragraph 4
perverse
(adj.) strange, contrary to what is reasonable or expected
perverso, contrario a la lógica
"It's a perverse system that pays for output, not results."
the wherewithal
(n.) the resources or means necessary for something
los medios necesarios
"Our team doesn't have the wherewithal to audit every vendor."

Some fixes are already underway: publishers are retracting compromised papers and dropping journals overrun by fake content. But most researchers agree the real fix has to happen further upstream — in how science funds and rewards its people. FAKE

Vocabulary — Paragraph 5
retract
(v.) to officially withdraw a statement or publication
retractarse, retirar una publicación
"The journal retracted the article after the fraud was confirmed."

🗣️ Discussion Questions

  • In your own field (EHS, HR, or IT), have you ever seen an incentive system reward quantity over quality? What was the effect?
  • Should companies or governments be responsible for detecting fraud, or should the burden fall on the people doing the reviewing? Why?
  • How much should a professional trust a report, study, or audit that "sounds plausible" but that they can't fully verify themselves?

📝 Self-Practice Questions

1. What's the difference between a paper being "plagiarized" and one being made up "from whole cloth"?
Plagiarism means stealing existing real work and passing it off as original. "Whole cloth" invention means there was no real work at all — the data, results, and sometimes even the experiment never existed.
2. Use "beset" and "laden" in two different sentences about your own workplace.
Example: "Our onboarding process is beset by outdated paperwork." / "The compliance folder was laden with unsigned forms." (Answers will vary — check that the grammar and meaning fit.)
3. Why might a hiring committee lack "the wherewithal" to properly judge a candidate's work?
They may lack time, technical expertise, or resources to check every detail closely, so they rely on shortcuts like counting publications, certifications, or years of experience instead.
4. Rewrite this sentence using "warped": "The company's values changed in a bad way because of years of dishonest reporting."
Example: "Years of dishonest reporting had warped the company's values." (Answers will vary, as long as "warped" is used correctly as a verb or adjective.)
