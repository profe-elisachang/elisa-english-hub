🌍 Engineering the Environment

A powerful approach to climate change raises powerful questions
by Karina Atkins / © 2024, Chicago Tribune. Distributed by Tribune Content Agency, LLC. PAGE DESIGN BY VICKY TSUNG

Could clouds be brightened so they reflect more sunlight back into outer space? If lab-grown seaweed is sunk into the ocean, how much carbon dioxide could it absorb? Would drilling holes into glaciers extract enough heat to slow sea level rise?

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 1

brighten

To make something lighter or more reflective

Scientists are studying how to brighten clouds to reflect more sunlight away from Earth.
absorb

To take in or soak up a substance or energy

absorber

The new filtration material can absorb up to 90% of the chemical waste in the water.

To remove or take out something from a source

extraer

The machine is designed to extract heat from underground rock formations.

While society is struggling to kick its addiction to fossil fuels, compensating by meddling with Earth's atmosphere, oceans and land masses has long been viewed as taboo. Many scientists have argued that geoengineering interventions are a distraction from emissions reductions at best and too dangerous to study at worst.

The most controversial, and likely also the fastest-acting method, is shooting aerosols into the sky to deflect the sun's rays, known as solar radiation management or solar geoengineering.

The process is anticipated to have a similar effect to massive volcanic eruptions such as the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora in modern-day Indonesia, which disrupted weather patterns globally for three years. Summer temperatures in Europe were the coldest on record and fog dimmed sunlight in the United States.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 2

meddle

To deliberately try to influence or change a situation that does not concern you, or that you do not understand

entrometerse / interferir

Engineers should not meddle with calibrated equipment without proper authorization.
geoengineering

Large-scale deliberate interventions in Earth's natural systems to counteract climate change

geoingeniería / ingeniería geográfica

Geoengineering proposals remain highly controversial because their side effects are unpredictable.
aerosol

Tiny particles or droplets suspended in the atmosphere, which can scatter sunlight

aerosol

Volcanic eruptions release natural aerosols that can temporarily cool global temperatures.
deflect

If someone or something deflects something that is moving, or if it deflects, it turns in a different direction

taboo

A social or cultural prohibition; something avoided because it is seen as unacceptable

anticipated

Expected or predicted to happen in the future

previsto / esperado

Could vs. should

But instead of volcanic ash, man-made aerosols would envelop the world and deflect the sun's rays. Temperatures worldwide would presumably start dropping within a year.

But scientists have historically kept geoengineering at arm's length out of concern that rich nations and fossil fuel companies would lean on promises of expensive, yet-to-be-developed technology to avoid changing their emissions-intensive practices. Carbon emissions are the root cause of climate change.

However, society is unlikely to cut emissions fast enough to stave off the worst of climate change, experts say.

The International Energy Agency anticipates that total mineral demand for clean energy technologies, from solar panels to EV batteries, will double or quadruple by 2040. Not only is mining emissions-intensive, but it also takes time.

[University of Chicago physicist Peter] Irvine said he's certain solar geoengineering would reduce extreme heat, heavy rainfall and sea-level rise. [But] he admits it may also intensify droughts, increase acid rain and thin the ozone layer. These unknowns could affect food production and biodiversity worldwide.

biodiversity

The variety of plant and animal life in a particular habitat or on Earth as a whole

Once started, sun-deflecting aerosols must be shot into the sky every couple of years. If stopped, the Earth could experience rapid warming, also known as "termination shock."

"It's an enormous burden on the future," said Raymond Pierrehumbert, a former University of Chicago professor who now works at Oxford University. "How do you know these interventions are going to be doable for the next generation? What if there's a global war, another pandemic?"

He is one of more than 500 scientists who have signed an open letter calling for an international nonuse agreement on solar geoengineering.

The signatories cite concern that there is no adequate governing body to determine fair and effective deployment of a technology that will require coordination between and touch every corner of the planet.

Irvine, however, cautions that ignoring solar geoengineering may increase the risk of it being used irresponsibly.

"The more it's suppressed, the greater the risk of a single nation or small group of nations pushing ahead independently in desperation," he said.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 4

governing body

Opening up the discussion

So far, most research has been limited to computer modeling. While valuable, unforeseen variables are likely to make real-world scenarios more complex. Field research that would begin exploring these complexities has been difficult to get approved. Two high-profile experiments—one in California and another in Sweden—were canceled in [2024] due to local opposition.

Residents, including a Nordic indigenous tribe, expressed passionate concerns about potential impacts on weather patterns.

[Physicist David] Keith, who was involved in the Sweden project, has spent his career guided by the conviction that the unknowns are all the more reason to study geoengineering. For decades, he has argued that politicians and the public can only decide whether interventions are worth deploying when armed with rigorous research.

"It's definitely not an elite club of scientists' job to decide what the public gets to know or not," Keith said. "I think that's deeply unethical."

"In the end, this needs to be global, we need to build a field," [he] said.

    ___ Very thorough and careful

Reference Answers
  1. termination shock
  2. False. The scientists signed a letter calling for a nonuse agreement—meaning they want an agreement NOT to use solar geoengineering, not to support it.
  3. c – suppress
    b – signatory
    d – rigorous
    a – stave off
  4. Scientists kept geoengineering at arm's length because: (1) they were concerned that rich nations and fossil fuel companies would use promises of this technology as an excuse to avoid cutting emissions; and (2) they considered it too dangerous to study due to the risk of unintended consequences such as intensified droughts, increased acid rain, and disruption of food production and biodiversity.
