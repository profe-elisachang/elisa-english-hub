📊 Article: The Cost of Extreme Heat Is Displacement
🌍 The Shrinking Space for Human Life
Every summer now brings headlines about record temperatures. But for millions of people, extreme heat isn't just an uncomfortable season — it's becoming a reason to leave home for good. Researchers describe something called the human climate niche: the narrow range of temperature and humidity in which human societies have always been able to thrive. As the planet warms, that safe zone is shrinking fast. One major scientific study warns that by 2070, as much as a third of humanity could be living outside the conditions that once made survival comfortable. Part of the danger comes from "wet-bulb temperature," a measurement that combines heat and humidity. Once this reaches a certain threshold — around 35°C — the human body can no longer cool itself, no matter how much someone sweats. Some cities have already crossed this dangerous line, and scientists now suspect the real survival limit may be even lower. But long before a place becomes uninhabitable, it can become economically and socially impossible to live in.

💸 When Staying Costs Too Much
Interestingly, people rarely leave home because of heat alone. They leave because heat quietly makes everyday life too expensive to sustain. Whether a family can stay in their community often comes down to whether they can afford to adapt — and that turns extreme heat into a force multiplier for existing inequality. Wealthier households can install air conditioning or temporarily relocate during a heat wave. Wealthier cities can invest in cooling infrastructure. But for billions of people, none of these options exist. Crops fail after repeated heat waves, and outdoor workers — in construction, farming, or street vending — lose income as dangerous temperatures shorten the hours they can safely work. Meanwhile, household budgets shrink even as spending on water, electricity, and healthcare rises. Pregnant women, young children, and older adults face the greatest health risks, especially when nights no longer cool down enough for the body to recover.

🌾 One Family, Two Years of Shocks
Consider the story of Santuben Kantibhai, a farmer in Gujarat, India. Over just two years, her family absorbed one shock after another. A severe heat wave destroyed most of their crop, cutting their income sharply. Around the same time, her father — who also earned wages through farm labor — began losing his eyesight to cataracts, making it harder for him to work. When another heat wave struck the following year, the family's harvest failed again, right as her father needed surgery. For Santuben, the question was never whether heat was becoming more dangerous — she already knew that. The real question was how many more failed harvests her family could absorb before leaving became the only option left.

🛠️ Solutions We Already Have
The encouraging part of this story is that solutions already exist. Trees can lower neighborhood temperatures by several degrees. Reflective "cool roofs," shaded streets, and naturally cool building designs can all reduce dangerous temperatures. Early warning systems save lives by giving people time to prepare. In Santuben's case, a financial safety net — a type of insurance tied to weather data — helped cushion her family's lost income and cover her father's medical costs, allowing him to return to work. That kind of support didn't just protect their finances; it protected their ability to choose whether to stay on their land at all. The real obstacle isn't a lack of solutions — it's a lack of resources. The communities most exposed to extreme heat are often the ones with the least money to invest in adapting to it.

⚖️ Heat Doesn't Affect Everyone Equally
Solutions also need to account for the fact that heat does not affect everyone equally. Along with income, factors like gender, race, disability, and legal status shape how much risk a person faces — and how much support they can access. Women, for example, already make up an estimated 80% of people displaced by climate-related disasters, since many carry a disproportionate share of unpaid caregiving, water collection, and cooking — responsibilities that don't pause during a heat wave. Leaving home, meanwhile, doesn't automatically mean safety. Migrants often face lethal temperatures during the journey itself, and some arrive only to find themselves working in the hottest, most dangerous jobs available. In 2021, an undocumented farmworker from Guatemala died while working outdoors in Oregon during a record heat wave, when the pavement grew soft beneath his feet. For him, heat simply followed him across the border.

📈 The Numbers — and the Choice Ahead
The World Bank estimates that, in a high-emissions future, as many as 216 million people could be displaced within their own countries over the next 25 years because of climate impacts. But the same research suggests that ambitious adaptation and cuts to emissions could reduce that number by as much as 80%. When people are forced to leave because of climate impacts, migration isn't a solution — it's proof that support arrived too late. We already have the knowledge to build cooler cities, protect outdoor workers, and cut the emissions driving temperatures higher. What's missing isn't know-how. It's the political will to act before the choice is made for us.
