🌱 From Despair to HOPE in Haiti

Making once-arid lands fertile and poor farmers into money-makers

by Jacqueline Charles / © 2023, The Miami Herald. Distributed by Tribune Content Agency, LLC. PAGE DESIGN BY OLIVE CHU

Cabbages and pumpkins rise out of the ground; papayas hang from trees; and workers plant rows of hot peppers in the freshly plowed dirt.

A year ago, such a lush landscape was unimaginable for Fransik Monchèr, a farmer and father of seven who couldn’t even grow fiery habanero peppers because they quickly died.

All that changed the day a group of entrepreneurs decided to take a gamble to launch a socioeconomic experiment with the goal of answering a simple but daunting question: What if a Haitian farmer, like Monchèr, had everything he needed to be a successful grower?

The quest for the answers has birthed an unusual partnership among four Port-au-Prince-based friends and entrepreneurs, and local farmers and agronomists in northern Haiti. Together, they are pushing locally grown peppers and sweet potatoes while also aiding farmers like Monchèr in transforming their land, restoring hope in the only livelihood they’ve known: agriculture.

Creating ‘The Haiti of Tomorrow’

Though 75% of Haiti’s population lives in rural areas, the country can’t feed itself. Nearly half of the population, 4.9 million people, are experiencing acute hunger, according to the United Nations.

Crops fail due to increasingly frequent and severe droughts and tropical storms and higher-than-average temperatures.

“Everybody is locked into the idea that it can’t be done,” said Geoffrey Handal, the accounting and logistics expert in the friends’ group. “We have all of the qualifications; we have all of the techniques needed; we have all of the land; we have all of the water; we have everything.”

Handal and [his friends] launched their experiment with Monchèr. They’ve dubbed their socioeconomic lab “Ayiti Demen”—The Haiti of Tomorrow.

Solving the problem of water

In Haiti, crops failed not just because of too little or too much rainfall, but also due to a lack of access to irrigation.

Just across the border, 45 minutes away, in the Dominican Republic, farmers are successful, [entrepreneur Maxwell] Marcelin said, so the issue is not the availability of water.

“The only thing is, there is no investment on our side of the border to bring the water to the producers,” he said.

Armed with a study showing that there was indeed water under Monchèr’s arid land, the group sprang into action. Handal provided the seeds. Marcelin crunched the numbers; he wanted to show Monchèr that he could have a successful harvest and rely on it for income to feed his family.

Since [then], Monchèr has grown more than 2,000 pounds of habanero peppers, in addition to other fruits and vegetables.

“They backed me up,” he said. “I could not have accomplished this on my own.”

Whereas before he saw despair, he now sees hope. “My children are starting to eat, and I am beginning to make some money,” Monchèr said.

The group’s initial investment is expected to bring Monchèr about $7,000 in sales, which he will make from selling his habanero peppers.

“This is someone we’ve taken out of poverty when we gave him this opportunity,” Marcelin said.

Having a stake in the country

“If we do this right, if we invest properly in Haiti, in 20 years, we can have 10% GDP growth every year. ... This is how you create a real economy,” [Handal said.]

To make the agricultural project work, the duo invested in a drip irrigation system and fertilizer. They also brought onboard interns from the nearby University of Limonade to assist and to learn.

“At the end of the day, when you look at the investment, it comes up to 50-50 in terms of profits,” Marcelin said. “We created jobs but the profit sharing is for the whole organization. ... Everyone who is in the ecosystem is making money.”

💡 in terms of = regarding / from the perspective of → en cuanto a / desde el punto de vista de
comes up to = reaches a total of → asciende a / alcanza

Handal also sees another important result: “For me as long as the community is involved, that’s all you need,” he said.

The effort is “a bet that this country will not die, which is a bit of a leap of faith these days. But if it’s not going to die, it’s going to grow at some point,” Handal said. “And that’s why we invest.”

Having a stake in the country means you care about its future because you are personally invested.

Bringing back the Scotch bonnet

The entrepreneurs also want to bring back production of the Caribbean Scotch bonnet, a variety of chili pepper. According to local lore, the Scotch bonnet, popular in Jamaica, was bountiful before the Haitian revolution but soon disappeared after Haiti won its independence from France in 1804.

Ingenuity

The [friends] also focus on finding creative ways to get around problems. After some of the habaneros and Piman Bouk ripened before they could be shipped out, Marcelin and Handal decided to go into the pepper sauce business.

💬 Discussion Questions

  • In your experience (EHS, IT, or Process Engineering), what kind of “investment” (resources, training, infrastructure) could turn a struggling team into a successful one?
  • Haitian entrepreneurs used a 50-50 profit-sharing model. Would a similar model work in your department? Why or why not?
  • Water access was the key bottleneck. In our operations, what is a “hidden bottleneck” people assume cannot be fixed, but you believe could be solved with the right approach?

✍️ Self-Practice Questions

  1. Fill in the blank: “After the safety audit, the EHS team _____________ and started corrective actions.” (spring into action / crunch the numbers)
  2. True or False? A “daunting” task feels easy and comfortable.
  3. Rewrite using “have a stake in”: Engineers who are personally invested in the project’s success suggest better ideas.
  4. Match each word: (1) quest  (2) drought  (3) backed me up → a) me apoyaron   b) sequía   c) búsqueda/misión
🔎 Reference Answers
  1. sprang into action
  2. False – daunting makes you feel worried or afraid.
  3. Engineers who have a stake in the project’s success suggest better ideas.
  4. 1-c, 2-b, 3-a
