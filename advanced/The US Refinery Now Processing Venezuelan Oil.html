Section 2

🌍 Background Knowledge

The Global Context

This article takes place during a major geopolitical shock: Iran has blocked the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which roughly 20% of the world's oil passes. When this route closes, global oil prices spike dramatically, similar to what happened during the 1973 Arab Oil Embargo and the 1990 Gulf War. The US is now under pressure to find alternative oil sources to keep prices from rising even further for American consumers.

Venezuela offers a solution: it holds the world's largest oil reserves. Under former president Nicolás Maduro, US sanctions had blocked Venezuelan oil exports entirely. But after a dramatic night-time military operation captured Maduro in January, the new government reopened oil exports. Chevron — the only US company with operations in Venezuela — moved quickly to take advantage.

Mexico's Position — More Complicated Than It Looks

Mexico produces oil. But here is the paradox that most people don't know: Mexico sells crude oil but buys back gasoline. PEMEX, the national oil company, lacks sufficient refining capacity to process its own crude into usable fuel. As a result, Mexico imports approximately 1.2 million barrels of refined petroleum products per day from the United States.

~$100B PEMEX total debt — world's most indebted energy company
1.64M barrels/day current Mexican production (declining since 2005)
7% of Mexican government revenue comes from oil sector
Mexico's Oil Paradox
Mexico extracts crude oil
→
Exports it (revenue)
→
PEMEX can't refine enough
→
Imports gasoline & diesel from the US
→
Sells to Mexican consumers
⚠️ This means when global oil prices rise, Mexico feels pain from both sides: export revenues may increase slightly, but the cost of importing refined fuel also rises — and PEMEX is too financially weak to benefit fully from higher crude prices.

What Venezuelan Oil Means for Mexico

Venezuela and Mexico both export heavy crude oil — thick, dark, sulfur-rich oil that requires specialized refineries to process. Most US Gulf Coast refineries, including Chevron's Pascagoula plant in this article, were specifically designed to handle heavy crude. This means Venezuela and Mexico are direct competitors for the same buyers.

When Venezuelan oil flows back into the US market, it increases total supply. In theory, more supply should mean lower prices — which sounds good. But for Mexico, more Venezuelan competition in the US market means downward pressure on the price PEMEX receives for its crude. Combined with PEMEX's already declining production and massive debt, this makes Mexico's fiscal situation more fragile.

For your factory specifically: lower government oil revenues → less government spending → potentially slower infrastructure investment → longer-term pressure on roads, ports, and utilities that your supply chain depends on.

The Key Tension in This Article

The article reveals a fundamental contradiction in oil economics that will help you understand many future news stories:

Even when you are surrounded by oil, you are not protected from high oil prices.
This is because oil is priced on global markets. A refinery in Mississippi pays the world price for crude — regardless of how close Venezuela or Mexico are. Trump's claim that "we have plenty of gas" does not automatically translate to cheaper prices at the pump. The market does not work that way.

📚 Key Vocabulary

These are the words you need to read this article fluently — and use in professional conversations about energy, supply chain, and business strategy.

Paragraphs 1–4 · The Ship & Venezuela's Oil
crude oil

Unrefined oil directly from the ground — before it is processed into gasoline, diesel, or other products.

petróleo crudo / crudo

⭐ Why it matters in business When you read financial news about oil prices (WTI, Brent), they are always quoting the price of crude oil — not gasoline. This distinction matters for understanding cost calculations.
"The refinery receives crude oil by ship and transforms it into usable fuel."
sanctions

Official penalties imposed by one country on another — usually blocking trade, freezing assets, or banning business dealings.

sanciones

⭐ Why it matters in business Sanctions can instantly block your company from doing business with a supplier or customer in another country. Understanding this word helps you follow geopolitical risk in global supply chains.
"US sanctions against Venezuela made it illegal to import Venezuelan oil until the political situation changed."
reserves

The total quantity of a natural resource (oil, gas, minerals) that is known to exist underground and can be economically extracted.

reservas

⭐ Why it matters in business "Reserves" appears in annual reports for mining, energy, and even automotive companies (battery material reserves). It signals long-term availability and strategic value.
"Venezuela has the world's largest oil reserves, but lack of investment has prevented it from extracting them efficiently."
Paragraphs 5–9 · The Refinery & Why Heavy Oil Matters
refinery

An industrial facility that processes crude oil into usable products such as gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and petrochemicals.

refinería

⭐ Why it matters in business Refineries are the critical bottleneck between raw oil and the fuel that powers factories and vehicles. Mexico's lack of refining capacity is a key reason for its energy vulnerability.
"Chevron's refinery in Mississippi was specifically built to process heavy crude oils like those from Venezuela."
incentive

A financial or strategic benefit that motivates a company or person to take a specific action.

incentivo

⭐ Why it matters in business This word appears constantly in business strategy, HR (performance incentives), and government policy (tax incentives for manufacturing investment). It is essential vocabulary for any business discussion.
"The government offered tax incentives to attract foreign automotive manufacturers to build plants in Mexico."
downstream

In the oil industry, the stage of operations involving refining crude oil and distributing the finished products. The opposite of "upstream" (exploration and extraction).

aguas abajo / sector downstream

⭐ Why it matters in business "Upstream" and "downstream" are also used in supply chain management outside the oil industry — upstream = suppliers, downstream = customers and distribution. You will hear this in automotive manufacturing discussions.
"The automotive company's downstream operations include vehicle distribution and after-sales service."
Paragraphs 10–15 · Prices & The Market Contradiction
susceptible to

Unable to avoid being affected or harmed by something; vulnerable to a risk or external force.

susceptible a / vulnerable a

⭐ Why it matters in business This phrase is essential when discussing risk management. "Our supply chain is susceptible to port delays" or "Our costs are susceptible to currency fluctuation" — it signals professional awareness of vulnerability.
"Even domestic manufacturers are susceptible to global commodity price changes."
fluctuations

Irregular and unpredictable changes in price, level, or value — up and down over time.

fluctuaciones

⭐ Why it matters in business One of the most important words in financial and operational reporting. "Currency fluctuations," "price fluctuations," "demand fluctuations" — this word appears in almost every business report and earnings call.
"The automotive plant's budget was affected by fluctuations in the price of steel and aluminum."
masking

Hiding or concealing the true effect of something — often a positive effect that is temporarily hidden by a negative one.

enmascarando / ocultando

⭐ Why it matters in business A sophisticated business vocabulary word. "The strong sales in Q3 were masking underlying cost problems" — this kind of analysis signals strategic thinking in executive meetings.
"The high demand from one customer was masking a broader decline in the company's market share."
