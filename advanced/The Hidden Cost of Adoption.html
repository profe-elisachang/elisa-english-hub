🧭 Background: Before You Read

📝 This article is an opinion piece written by an adoptive parent. It argues one side and does not explain how adoption systems work. Read it as an argument, not as a neutral report.
1. Four different paths to adoption
Child welfare system (foster care) → adoption
Sistema de protección infantil → adopción
How it happens
The government removes a child from the home. If the child cannot return, a court ends the parents’ legal rights and the child may be adopted.
Parents’ situation
Ranges from serious abuse to poverty, unstable housing or drug addiction.
Voluntary relinquishment
Entrega voluntaria en adopción
How it happens
Birth parents give up a child, often a baby, through an agency or a private arrangement.
Parents’ situation
The author argues that this “choice” is often shaped by poverty or lack of support.
International adoption
Adopción internacional
How it happens
A child is adopted by a family in another country, usually through an agency.
Why it is controversial
The article says weak regulations have allowed agencies to take children by force or pressure. Some countries have banned it.
Kinship adoption
Adopción por familiares o allegados
How it happens
Relatives or close friends adopt the child.
Why the author prefers it
The child stays inside the biological family’s network, so ties are kept.
2. Three words that are easy to confuse
Abuse
Maltrato / abuso
Actively harming a child, physically, sexually or emotionally. Almost everyone agrees this can justify removal.
Neglect
Negligencia / desatención
Failing to meet a child’s basic needs: food, supervision, medical care, safe housing. It can be serious and ongoing, or it can be a short-term situation.
Poverty
Pobreza
Lacking money and resources. It makes neglect more likely, but it is not the same thing. A poor family can be loving and safe; a wealthy family can still neglect a child.
⚖️ The core tension: two things can be true at the same time.
  • Some parents truly cannot keep a child safe, and removal is necessary.
  • Some removals happen because a family is poor or unsupported, not because the child is in danger. The author wants these families helped, not separated.
Note: In the United States, neglect is the most common reason children are removed from their homes, and experts debate how often it is really poverty. Rules and numbers differ from country to country, and the article does not name a country.

⚖️ Article

👶 The Hidden Cost of Adoption

Why adoption policies need to change

1 People usually portray adoption as a happy ending to a hard start in life: a child becomes legally part of a new family. Having built my own family this way, I know it can be deeply fulfilling, bringing together people who want a child and a child who needs a home. Yet society tends to call adopted children “lucky,” and that label overlooks the harder realities. Often adoption is rooted in poverty, weak protection for families, or a failure of social support. I am grateful for the children in my family, but we must also admit that adoption has lifelong consequences for the child.

2 However much love an adoptive family gives, it cannot erase the loss of the child’s biological family. Research indicates that cutting an infant or child off from the mother is traumatic because it disrupts the earliest bonds. Adopted children often struggle with social and emotional development, especially attachment. Afraid of being rejected again, many ask, “Why did my parents leave me?” or “If they left, will you leave me too?” This difficulty trusting and bonding can shape not only the relationship with parents but also friendships and romantic partnerships later in life.

3 Shame and guilt are also common. Adopted people may feel ashamed of where they come from, or feel guilty for missing their first family even though their adoptive parents love them. Birthdays, holidays and other milestones remind them of who is absent, yet they often grieve alone because they are afraid of upsetting their adoptive family.

4 Adoptees also wrestle with questions of belonging and identity. Most children share genes, history and cultural heritage with their relatives, but adoptees may feel alienated because they do not share biology and culture with their family. When they have no information about their family background, heritage or medical history, even basic questions about who they are can be hard to answer.

5 Many adoptees are now speaking out. They urge society to try every other option before resorting to adoption. Because separation does so much harm, they call for stronger social support so that families can stay together. Where keeping the family intact is simply not possible, they see kinship adoption (adoption by relatives or close friends) as the best route, since it helps keep biological ties alive.

6 The author also argues for tighter oversight of adoption systems. In recent years, countries across the Americas, Europe, Africa, Asia and Australia have admitted that, as long as regulations stay weak, agencies will kidnap children or remove them by coercive means to supply both domestic and international adoption. In response, some governments have banned international adoption and favor domestic adoption in order to protect cultural ties. At the same time, open adoption, in which adoptees stay in touch with their biological parents, is becoming more common as societies try to lessen the harm of separation.

7 Adoption, although usually carried out with the best intentions, comes at a high emotional cost. If society learns from what adoptees have lived through and gives better support to struggling families, it can protect some of its most vulnerable members.

🗣️ Discussion Questions

1The author says calling adopted children “lucky” overlooks the harder realities. Where else do we see a “happy” label hiding a more complicated truth, such as in the workplace or in our own culture?
2The article suggests that stronger social support for struggling families could reduce the need for adoption. How far should governments and companies go in supporting families, and who should pay for it?
3Some countries have banned international adoption to protect cultural ties, while others keep it open. What are the strongest arguments on each side, and which concerns do you think should come first?
4The article does not say how to tell a family in poverty from a family where a child is in danger. What signs or evidence would you want decision-makers to look at before separating a child from their parents?
5The author is an adoptive parent writing an opinion piece, with no statistics cited. What would make you trust the argument more, and what kinds of voices or data are missing?
6How is adoption talked about in Mexico, in Taiwan, or in your own family and workplace? Is it treated mostly as a happy ending, as a last resort, or as something people avoid discussing?
7Imagine you advise a government with a limited budget. Would you fund (a) support for struggling families, (b) stricter oversight of adoption agencies, or (c) more kinship placements first? Explain your choice and respond to the strongest objection to it.

✏️ Self-Practice Questions

1. Which sentence correctly uses “be rooted in”?
a) The workers were rooted in the factory all night.
b) The accident was rooted in poor communication between shifts.
c) She rooted in the new project very quickly.

Answer: b. “Be rooted in” describes the basic cause or origin of something (a problem, a belief, a tradition). It is not used for people physically staying somewhere.
2. Complete with the correct word: “Several employees decided to ______ about the unsafe conditions.” (speak out / speak up for / speak over)
Answer: speak out. “Speak out” means to say publicly what you think about a problem. (“Speak up” is also possible, but it often means to talk louder or to raise a concern in a smaller setting.)
3. Two meanings: In the article, “oversight” means careful supervision. Can you explain a sentence where it means something different?
Possible answer: “The missing signature was an oversight; nobody noticed it before the audit.” Here “oversight” means a mistake made by failing to notice something. Context tells you which meaning is intended.
4. Rewrite in your own words: “Adoption, though often done with the best intentions, comes at a high emotional cost.”
Model answer: “Even when people adopt a child out of good motives, the child often pays a heavy emotional price.”
Pattern to reuse: “[Action], though often done with the best intentions, comes at a high cost.” Try it with a workplace topic such as strict monitoring, overtime or automation.
