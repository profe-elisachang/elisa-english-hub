The philosophical roots of empathy skepticism

The general idea of being moved by others' suffering has been a subject of philosophical attention for millennia, under labels such as "pity," "sympathy" and "compassion."

One of the earliest warnings about pity in Western philosophy came from the Greek Stoic philosopher Epictetus. Epictetus believed that you should want for others what fate has in store for them, whether good or bad. Feeling pity for them would be unpleasant, and nobody would choose them for themselves. Empathy would clearly fall into this category, keeping us from living the good life.

A similar objection emerged much later from the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche. Like Epictetus, Nietzsche worried that pity or compassion was a burden on the individual, preventing them from living the good life. Nietzsche warns that such feelings could impair the very people who try to help others.

Recall the phenomenon of empathy fatigue. One psychological explanation for why empathic people experience fatigue and even burnout is that empathy involves a kind of mirroring of other people's mental life, a mirroring that can be physically unpleasant. When someone you love is in pain, you don't just believe that they are in pain; you may feel it as if it is actually happening to you.

Empathy is thus difficult to bear precisely because being in pain is difficult to bear.

Understanding knowledge

The answer for why one should see empathy as a strength starts with a key insight from a famous thought experiment by the Australian philosopher Frank Jackson.

Imagine a scientist named Mary who has studied colors despite having lived her entire life in a black and white room. She knows all the facts about the spectrum distribution of light sources and vision science. She's read descriptions of the redness of roses and azaleas. But she's never seen color herself. Does Mary know everything about redness? Many epistemologists—people who study the nature of knowledge—argue that she does not.

What Mary learns when she sees red for the first time is elusive. If she returns to her black and white room, never to see any colored objects again, her knowledge of the color red will likely diminish over time. To have a full, rich understanding of colors, one needs to experience them.

Appreciating empathy

People can become less familiar with pain and hardship during times when things are going well for them. All of us can temporarily lose the rich experiential grasp of what it is like to be distressed. So, when we consider the pain and suffering of others in the abstract or without directly feeling it, it is very much like trying to grasp the nature of redness while being personally acquainted only with a field of black and white.

That is where empathy comes in. Through experiential simulation of another's feelings, empathy affords us a rich grasp of the distress that others feel. It affords us a more accurate understanding of others' experiences and emotions. Empathy is thus a form of knowledge that can be hard to bear, just as pain can be hard to bear. But that's precisely why empathy, properly cultivated, is a strength. Resisting empathy impairs our decision-making. It keeps us from understanding what's at stake.

🎯 Specialized Terms

Key Academic & Philosophical Vocabulary

empathy (n)
['empəθi]
The ability to understand and share the feelings of another person. It means putting yourself in someone else's position to feel what they feel.
skepticism (n)
['skeptɪsɪzəm]
An attitude of doubting that particular claims or statements are true or that something will happen. It means questioning whether something is really accurate or valid.
Stoicism (n)
['stoʊɪsɪzəm]
An ancient Greek philosophy that teaches people to accept whatever happens calmly without showing strong emotions. Stoics believed in accepting fate and controlling their reactions.
progressive (n)
[prə'gresɪv]
A person who supports or advocates for social reform and new ideas, especially in politics. Progressives typically favor change that they believe will improve society.
millennium (n)
[mɪ'leniəm]
A period of 1,000 years. For example, the year 2000 marked the beginning of a new millennium.
experiential (adj)
[ɪk,spɪri'enʃəl]
Based on experience or observation rather than theory or book learning. Experiential knowledge comes from actually doing or experiencing something yourself.
distressed (adj)
[dɪ'strest]
Very upset, worried, or experiencing anxiety and suffering. When someone is distressed, they are in emotional or mental pain.
simulation (n)
[,sɪmjə'leɪʃən]
The act of imitating or copying a real situation, often to test something or understand how it works. A simulation creates conditions similar to reality for practice or study.

While empathy can be hard to bear (like pain is hard to bear), when properly cultivated, it prevents us from making poor decisions and enables us to better understand the impact of our actions on others.
