🔍 2. Article: Gateway Products and the Path of Innovation

🚪 Gateway Products: The First Step Toward Something Else

Many important innovations do not immediately replace an entire system. Instead, they solve a problem within the limits of the technology available at the time. A portable video recorder, for example, gave ordinary people a convenient way to record moments without carrying professional equipment. Similar patterns appeared with early personal digital assistants, online mapping services, and other technologies that later gave way to more integrated solutions.

These products can be understood as gateway products. They create value because they give customers something useful today while also revealing what customers will eventually expect tomorrow.

🧩 Problem Solved? That Does Not Mean the Product Will Last

A gateway product is not necessarily a failed product. In fact, many of them solve genuine customer problems extremely well. The strategic issue is that the solution may depend on a larger system that eventually becomes unnecessary.

Consider the fax machine. It dramatically reduced the time required to send information compared with physical mail. But the experience still depended on paper, telephone lines, fax numbers, and dedicated equipment. Email eventually removed much of that scaffolding. The fax machine solved the problem of slow document delivery, but it did not eliminate the infrastructure behind the problem.

🖥️ When “Not Yet Good Enough” Creates a Market

Sometimes a dedicated product becomes successful precisely because the larger technology is not yet good enough. Before smartphones became capable cameras, portable digital cameras offered better image quality and easier handling. Before phones provided reliable navigation, stand-alone GPS units filled the gap.

The important question is not simply whether the product works. It is: what temporary limitation in another technology is this product exploiting? Once that limitation disappears, the product may lose its reason for existing.

🧠 Customer Learning Can Become a Barrier

Technology alone does not determine whether customers switch. Human behavior matters too. Customers who spend years learning a product may become highly skilled at using it. They may know shortcuts, workarounds, and specialized functions that make them efficient.

This creates a powerful form of resistance. Customers have already invested time, money, and effort in the old system. Economists call these previous investments sunk costs. As a result, people may continue using a product not because it is objectively the best option, but because switching feels expensive.

For businesses, this creates an unusual form of customer retention: loyalty may depend on what customers have learned rather than what they actually love.

📉 The Technology Gap Does Not Last Forever

This leads to one of the most important strategic questions: What temporary technological gap is creating demand for your product?

A product may exist because another technology cannot yet perform a particular task well enough. But technological progress gradually closes that gap. When that happens, customers may no longer need a separate solution.

Companies therefore need to monitor the gap relentlessly. The relevant question is not only how large the market is today, but also whether the underlying reason for that market will still exist tomorrow.

⚠️ What Happens When the Gap Closes?

This may be the question that business leaders most often avoid. If a company has built a profitable business around a temporary technological limitation, what happens when that limitation disappears?

Acknowledging that your product has an expiration date can feel like admitting failure. Yet the alternative can be more dangerous. Managers may continue investing in an old business because current revenue looks healthy.

Strong short-term performance can therefore lure managers into believing that the current business model is permanent. They may overinvest in extending the product's life instead of preparing the next solution.

🚪 Is a Gateway Product a Bad Business?

Not necessarily. A gateway product can be an excellent business if the company understands what role the product plays. Its value may not come from lasting forever. Instead, the product can help a company learn about customer behavior, build relationships, develop capabilities, and understand an emerging market.

The danger appears when a company treats the gateway as a permanent destination. A temporary bridge can still be extremely valuable. The strategic mistake is failing to build the next bridge.

The most resilient companies therefore ask not only, “How can we extend the life of this product?” but also, “What are customers learning from us, and what will they need next?”

💡 The Strategic Idea: A Gateway Is Valuable — But It Is Not the Destination

The article does not argue that gateway products are bad businesses. The deeper argument is about how companies interpret their success.

Customer problem → Gateway product → Customer learning → Technology improves → Gap closes → Next solution

A company becomes vulnerable when it focuses only on the product in the middle and ignores what comes next.

Strategic question: Is your product solving a permanent customer problem, or is it temporarily filling a gap that technology will eventually close?

🧩 4. Self-Practice Questions

1. Explain the difference between a “gateway product” and a “destination product.”
A gateway product helps customers move toward another technology, service, or market. A destination product is intended to remain the long-term solution rather than serving as a temporary bridge.
2. Why can sunk costs make customers reluctant to switch?
Customers may have invested significant time, money, training, and effort in the existing system. Even if a new product is objectively better, the perceived cost of abandoning the old system can discourage switching.
3. Complete the sentence: “A company should monitor its technological gap because ______.”
Possible answers:

• the gap may eventually disappear;
• competitors may develop a better solution;
• customer expectations may change;
• the existing product may become obsolete.
4. Explain this statement in your own words: “A gateway can be valuable without being permanent.”
A product can create significant business value even if customers will eventually move to another technology. The important point is whether the company understands the product's role and prepares for what customers will need next.
