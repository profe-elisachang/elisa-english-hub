🌱 Trauma-Informed Leadership

Why emotional safety is a measurable business advantage

by Mathangi Swaminathan / © 2025, Fast Company. Mansueto Ventures, LLC. Distributed by Tribune Content Agency, LLC.

📖 Article & Vocabulary

A decade ago, I joined a tech company in my first business development role in Singapore. Within the first quarter, I had closed two quarters' worth of sales targets. But the environment was abusive. The CEO yelled regularly. Personal and sexist remarks were common, on body, appearance, even what women ate or wore.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 1

abusive

The supervisor's abusive comments during the shift review created a hostile atmosphere on the production floor.

It was triggering. Having lived through a previous abusive situation, I found myself in constant flight-or-freeze mode. Every time I saw an email from my manager, my heart raced. I struggled to breathe in meetings. Despite my outward success, internally I was unraveling. Finally, I quit.

After weeks of double shifts, he felt himself unraveling under the pressure of the production deadline.

That experience changed the course of my career. For the next 10 years, I delved into how trauma shows up in people and organizations and eventually founded a global social enterprise focused on resilience-centered leadership.

More Than Performance Management

While HR manuals continue to discuss things like "performance management," what's often missed is the fact that people will escape environments where emotional strain is ignored or misunderstood. At the center of this gap is something we rarely train for: trauma literacy.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 4

literacy

knowledge and understanding of a particular subject

conocimiento y dominio de un tema

Improving safety literacy among new hires reduces accidents on the shop floor.

Trauma literacy is the ability (1) to recognize that unhealed past experiences show up in daily behavior and (2) to respond in ways that foster safety and resilience. You don't need to know someone's history to be mindful of trauma's effects. You just need to assume that trauma exists, and that it may be shaping how people show up at work.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 5

foster

to encourage the development or growth of something

fomentar

Clear communication channels foster trust between operators and management.

Managers often misinterpret the signs. Silence gets labeled as disinterest; anxiety looks like incompetence; over-functioning is praised until collapse. In reality, these are often trauma responses: fight, flight, freeze or fawn.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 6

over-functioning

doing more than is healthy or necessary, often to compensate for others

sobreesforzarse, hacer más de lo necesario

When a teammate is absent, over-functioning to cover their tasks can lead to burnout.
fawn

to praise someone insincerely, because you want them to like you or give you something

adular, halagar de forma poco sincera

Some employees fawn over supervisors during audits instead of raising real concerns.

The Business Cost of Trauma

Most workers are taught to push harder instead of pausing to hold space for emotions. Emotions are messy, and it often feels safer to stick with technical tasks and leave feelings unaddressed.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 7

hold space

to give someone emotional support and attention without judging them

dar espacio, apoyar emocionalmente

A good line leader knows how to hold space for a technician who made a costly mistake.

But a Harvard Business Review study found that employees who feel psychologically safe show a 76% increase in engagement, a 50% increase in retention, and a 67% increase in referrals. Trauma literacy is not "extra" — it's essential.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 8

retention

a company's ability to keep its employees from leaving to work elsewhere

retención de personal

Offering flexible schedules has improved retention among assembly line workers.

Here are trauma-informed practices any manager can implement.

① Acknowledge feelings

Start meetings with an honest check-in: not a general "How's everyone doing?" but "How are you, truly?" Emotions offer real-time information about morale.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 9

check-in

a brief conversation to see how someone is doing

reunión breve de seguimiento

The shift supervisor does a quick check-in with each operator before the line starts.

Of course, people won't open up just because you ask a deeper question. You need to create the conditions where it feels safe to answer honestly. That starts with you. As a manager, model emotional transparency in small, low-risk ways. Say things like, "I'm feeling a bit scattered today, but I'm here," or "I had a tough morning, so I might be quieter than usual." This signals that real emotions, not just polished updates, are welcome.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 10

open up

to start sharing personal feelings honestly

abrirse, sincerarse

It took months before the new hire felt comfortable enough to open up about the pressure of meeting quotas.
emotional transparency

being open and honest about your own feelings, keeping an open and sincere state

transparencia emocional

The plant manager's emotional transparency during the layoffs helped the team trust the process.
scattered

not clearly thinking or focused

disperso/a, desconcentrado/a

After the power outage, the whole maintenance team felt scattered and behind schedule.

② Don't try to fix them

Once someone shares something vulnerable, don't rush to fix it or dismiss it. Just reflect it back: "Thanks for sharing that, I hear you," or "That makes a lot of sense." From there, you might ask, "Is there anything you need from me today?" or "Would it help to adjust your workload this week?" You don't need to solve every emotional need. Trauma literacy isn't about absorbing everyone's pain. In fact, it's the opposite: Effective leadership requires responding to emotions without becoming consumed by them.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 11

reflect it back

to repeat back what someone said or felt, to show that you understand them

reflejar lo que se dijo

Instead of offering advice right away, she reflected back what the technician had said about feeling overwhelmed.
consumed by

overwhelmed or completely occupied by something

consumido/a por algo

A supervisor shouldn't become consumed by every complaint from the production floor.

③ Ask simple, effective questions

The 5W1H method is another great way to explore challenging moments. It stands for six simple but powerful questions to ask: what, why, when, where, who and how. For example, "What part of this task felt unclear?" or "When did you start feeling stuck?" These open-ended prompts help team members reflect and problem solve without feeling interrogated or blamed.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 12

interrogated

questioned intensely, sometimes using threats, in order to get information

interrogado/a

New operators shouldn't feel interrogated when reporting a machine malfunction.

④ Check how people are feeling

Trauma literacy isn't a one-off conversation; it's a culture. Build in rituals for reflection; adjust workloads proactively; and allocate time and resources toward psychological safety. That might mean adding five-minute "emotion check-outs" at the end of meetings, especially after intense sprints, high-stakes conversations, or moments of team transition, where each person shares how they're leaving (energized, drained, hopeful, unsure, etc.). If multiple people are feeling anxious or exhausted, that's a signal to adjust pace, revisit priorities, or check in one-on-one.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 13

allocate

to use something, or give it to someone, for a particular purpose

asignar

The plant manager allocated extra time for training after the safety incident.
sprints

short, intense periods of focused work

períodos intensos y enfocados de trabajo

The team just finished a production sprint to meet the client's shipping deadline.
