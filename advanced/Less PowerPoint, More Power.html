🎤 Less PowerPoint, More Power

Lead the room; don't read to it
Based on an article by Judith Humphrey — Fast Company, 2025  |  Read the full original article as handed out / projected in class
🗝️ Useful Expressions
lose touch with (someone)
perder el contacto / la conexión con
"The speaker completely lost touch with his audience."
drive home a point
recalcar o dejar muy claro un punto
"A single strong chart can drive home a point better than ten slides."
keep something front and center
mantener algo en primer plano / como prioridad
"Keep your key message front and center throughout the talk."
bring something to life
darle vida a algo
"Strong body language brings a presentation to life."
a call to action
un llamado a la acción
"End every proposal with a clear call to action."
📊 Article Overview
The Story: A Presentation Gone Wrong
The writer once sat through a painfully dull presentation. The speaker moved through back-to-back slides with no real connection to the room — and by the end, the audience had quietly filed out without him even noticing. The takeaway: slides can drain the power right out of a strong speaker.
back-to-back
(adj) happening one right after another, without a break
uno tras otro / consecutivo
"We had three back-to-back meetings this morning."
file (out)
(v) to walk somewhere, one after another, in a line
salir/entrar en fila
"Employees filed out of the auditorium after the safety drill."
1. Avoid Slides Whenever Possible
Before building a single slide, the author suggests asking whether you need one at all. A talk should persuade and inspire — something a deck full of visual props rarely does on its own. Sometimes the most powerful presentation is the one with no visuals at all.
prop
(n) something used to create or support a particular effect
accesorio / apoyo visual
"He used the safety helmet as a prop during the training."
deck
(n) a set of slides presented as a slideshow
conjunto de diapositivas
"Can you send me the deck before the meeting?"
2. Realize That You're the Best Visual
You are always more compelling than any inanimate image on a screen. Lead with your own energy and conviction instead of hiding behind your visuals — busy, cluttered slides are exactly what make audiences tune out.
inanimate
(adj) not living; without life
inanimado
"A slide is an inanimate object — it can't make eye contact with the audience."
tune (someone) out
(phr v) to ignore or stop paying attention to someone/something
dejar de prestar atención / desconectarse
"When the slides are too crowded, people tune out."
3. Make Sure You Have One Clear, Compelling Message
A strong presentation needs a single, memorable message, stated early and kept front and center the whole way through — the way Steve Jobs repeated one line when he first introduced the iPhone.
front and center
(adj/adv) in a very important, highly visible position
en primer plano / al centro de la atención
"Keep the client's needs front and center in every proposal."
4. Present with Great Conviction
Using slides doesn't lessen the need for real commitment — it actually doubles it. You have to own every visual: narrate it with confidence, and let your voice and body language show the audience that you mean what you're saying.
lessen
(v) to become smaller in size, importance, or value; to reduce
disminuir
"Good preparation lessens the risk of accidents on site."
own
(v) to manage or present something in a way that shows confidence and pride
apropiarse de / dominar con seguridad
"She owned the stage the moment she started speaking."
5. A Call to Action
Always close by echoing your opening message and telling the audience exactly what you want from them — whether that's approval, agreement, or full buy-in.
buy-in
(n) agreement and willingness to participate in or support a plan
aceptación / compromiso con un plan
"We need buy-in from every department before we roll out the new system."
💬 Discussion Questions
  • Think of the most boring presentation you've ever sat through. What exactly made it so hard to stay engaged?
  • Do you agree that "you are the best visual" in a presentation? Why or why not?
  • In your own job, when is it truly necessary to use slides, and when could you present just as well — or better — without them?
🎭 Role-Play Scenarios
EHS
You need to present a new safety protocol to plant managers who are tired of long slide decks. Practice opening with one clear, compelling message and keeping it front and center throughout.
HR
You're introducing a new benefits package to employees. Practice "owning" your message with conviction, and close with a call to action that gets real buy-in.
IT
You're proposing a system migration to leadership. Try presenting the plan with almost no slides — just your voice, your conviction, and one strong prop if needed.
✍️ Self-Practice Questions
Tap a question to reveal a sample answer. (Great as a warm-up at the start of next class!)
1. What's the difference between presenting to persuade and presenting to inform?
Sample answer: Persuading means you want the audience to feel or do something — to trust you, agree with you, or take action. Informing just means transferring facts. A great presentation usually leads with persuasion and uses information to support it, not the other way around.
2. Describe a time you had to "own" a message in front of others.
Sample answer: In my last performance review, I had to own the results of a project that didn't go as planned. Instead of hiding behind data, I explained clearly what happened and what I would do differently — and my manager respected the honesty.
3. What could you cut from your last presentation to make the message clearer?
Sample answer: I could cut at least half of my slides — most of them repeated information I was already saying out loud. A cleaner deck with one message per slide would have kept people's attention much longer.
4. Write one strong call to action you could use at the end of your next work presentation.
Sample answer: "I'm asking each of you to review the new protocol by Friday and send me your sign-off — that's how we get this rolled out by next month."
