🧠 The ADHD Advantage

The ADHD Advantage: Redefining Strengths and Struggles
Based on reporting by Jared Lindzon for Fast Company (2026) — read together in class from the original article.

In 2010, psychiatrist Dale Archer wrote a bestselling book celebrating the hidden strengths of ADHD. His later book, The ADHD Advantage, argued that the condition exists on a continuum. Archer's hypothesis is that people who score low may not even realize they have it, while those who score high often struggle daily and may need medication. Interestingly, many top performers — from Olympic swimmer Michael Phelps to comedian Howie Mandel and entrepreneur David Neeleman — openly credit ADHD for part of their success. Some describe how they hyperfocus intensely on projects that genuinely interest them, achieving remarkable results.

hypothesis
an idea proposed as a possible explanation, not yet proven
hipótesis
"Her hypothesis was that flexible hours improve safety compliance."
continuum
a scale of things that change gradually, with no clear separate parts
continuo / espectro
"Personality traits often exist on a continuum."
credit ... for
to say that someone/something is responsible for a good result
atribuir el mérito a
"He credits his team for the project's success."
hyperfocus
to concentrate so intensely that you lose track of everything else
hiperconcentrarse
"She hyperfocuses on projects she's passionate about."

Even so, ADHD remains full of contradictions. Some people feel almost paralyzed by mundane, everyday tasks and struggle in traditional school or office settings — they may get into more accidents and face higher rates of anxiety or depression. Others are unusually creative, entrepreneurial, and intuitive; some of the world's highest achievers, including Simone Biles and Richard Branson, say ADHD fuels their success. While our understanding of the condition has come a long way, researchers admit there is still a lot we don't know.

mundane
ordinary and not interesting
mundano / rutinario
"He gets bored with mundane office tasks."
get into
to become involved in something, often unintentionally
verse envuelto en
"She got into an argument with a coworker."
come a long way
to have made a lot of progress
haber avanzado mucho
"Workplace accommodations have come a long way."

Psychotherapist Sarah Greenberg describes ADHD brains as a mixed bag: the same person can overperform in one area and underperform in another. She might interview a brilliant, well-liked manager who overperforms at building relationships but underperforms at simple tasks like replying to texts or remembering birthdays. That's why Greenberg encourages coworkers, friends, and family members not to take these inconsistencies personally.

a mixed bag
a group of things that are very different; a combination of good and bad
una mezcla de cosas buenas y malas
"The reviews were a mixed bag."
overperform
to do better than expected
rendir por encima de lo esperado
"The new hire overperformed in her first month."
underperform
to do worse than expected
rendir por debajo de lo esperado
"Sales underperformed this quarter."

There is growing agreement that more support is needed. "How disabled we are happens at the intersection between our impairment and our environment," explains psychologist Megan Anna Neff. Most schools and workplaces were not designed with ADHD brains in mind, which limits access to the accommodations many people need to succeed.

intersection
the point where two things meet or overlap
intersección / cruce
"The policy sits at the intersection of safety and cost."
impairment
a condition in which part of the body or mind does not function typically
deficiencia
"The accommodation addressed his visual impairment."
access to
the ability to use or obtain something
acceso a
"All employees should have access to training."

Neff believes ADHD should officially classify as a disability, so that people who need extra resources and legal protections can continue to access them. At the same time, she recognizes that many people with ADHD don't consider themselves disabled and don't want to be labelled that way. When workplaces build environments that leverage a person's natural strengths — instead of only managing their weaknesses — "powerful things" can happen, she says.

classify ... as
to officially put something into a category
clasificar como
"The company classifies remote work as a benefit."
label
to describe someone with a word, sometimes unfairly
etiquetar
"She doesn't like to be labelled as 'the difficult one.'"
leverage
to use something to gain an advantage
aprovechar
"The manager leveraged her network to close the deal."

Neff herself has learned to channel her divergent, out-of-the-box thinking into her career as a psychologist, and she says she thrives with both ADHD and autism because she has control over her own work environment. Greenberg's story is similar: she struggled in traditional academic settings but built a successful career as a freelance journalist and author — not despite her ADHD, but because of it.

channel ... into
to direct energy or effort toward one particular purpose
canalizar hacia
"He channels his frustration into exercise."
out-of-the-box
creative, not following traditional methods
fuera de lo convencional
"The team came up with an out-of-the-box solution."
thrive
to grow, develop, or succeed very well
prosperar / destacar
"She thrives under pressure."
despite
without being affected by; even though
a pesar de
"Despite the rain, the event was a success."

🗣️ Discussion Questions

  • In your workplace, do you think environments and processes are built for a variety of brains, or more of a "one-size-fits-all" model? Give an example.
  • Neff says "how disabled we are happens at the intersection between our impairment and our environment." Do you agree? What could a workplace change to reduce that gap?
  • Greenberg believes her ADHD traits are directly responsible for her success as a journalist, not despite her ADHD. Do you think professional strengths and weaknesses are often two sides of the same coin — in any person, not just those with ADHD?

✍️ Self-Practice Questions

1. What is the "continuum" model of ADHD that Archer proposes? ▼
Archer suggests ADHD exists on a 10-point scale: people who score low (around 4 or below) may not even realize they have it, while people who score high (9 or above) tend to struggle more in daily life and may need medication.
2. Why does Neff believe ADHD should be classified as a disability? ▼
So that people who need extra resources, workplace accommodations, and legal protections can continue to have access to them — even though not everyone with ADHD sees themselves as disabled.
3. Name two challenges and two strengths associated with ADHD mentioned in the article. ▼
Challenges: struggling with mundane tasks, higher rates of anxiety/accidents, inconsistent performance (overperforming/underperforming). Strengths: creativity, hyperfocus, out-of-the-box/divergent thinking, entrepreneurial drive.
4. What does "leverage" mean in the context of this article? ▼
To use something — in this case, a person's natural strengths or traits — to gain an advantage, rather than only focusing on the weaknesses.
