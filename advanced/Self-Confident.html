😎 Are You Self-Confident Enough?

A trick for finding the balance between underselling and overselling

by Jessica Stillman · Inc., Mansueto Ventures LLC (2024), distributed by Tribune Content Agency

😬 Confidence or Cockiness?

Confidence and cockiness can look similar from the outside — but where exactly is the line between them? Confidence is surprisingly hard to define. Research on the topic sends mixed signals: one study finds that confidence pays off, even when it isn't backed up by real ability, while another warns that outright bragging tends to backfire, damaging how people see you.

Gender adds another layer of complexity. Multiple studies show that women are held to a stricter standard than men: the exact same level of self-promotion that seems normal — or even necessary — for a man is often judged as excessive for a woman.

So if you're an entrepreneur trying to promote your business, how do you know when you're pitching yourself hard enough — without crossing the line into arrogance or slimy self-promotion? One well-known venture capitalist has a simple test that can help.

cockiness 🔊n.
Excessive, overconfident self-assurance that comes across as arrogant.
ES: arrogancia, chulería
"His cockiness in the interview made the panel wonder if he'd take feedback well."
backfire 🔊v.
To produce the opposite result from the one you intended.
ES: salir el tiro por la culata
"Skipping the code review to save time backfired badly."
slimy 🔊adj.
Describes a person or manner that seems friendly but feels dishonest or manipulative.
ES: rastrero, empalagoso
"Employees don't trust him — his compliments always feel slimy."

🍽️ The Dinner Party Test

Andrew Chen, a partner at the venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz, has written about exactly this problem. In his experience, founders usually go wrong in one direction: they undersell how impressive they and their companies really are, afraid that too much confidence will scare off investors.

According to Chen, most founders default to talking almost entirely about their idea itself, to the exclusion of everything else — the team, the momentum, the credentials behind the project. As a result, they downplay their own achievements, or leave them out completely.

To check whether you're pitching yourself with enough confidence, Chen suggests a simple thought experiment: the "dinner party test." Picture yourself saying your credentials and achievements out loud — but at a casual dinner party, not an investor meeting. "If you are pitching yourself hard, then if you are a kind human, you will turn red and blush with wild embarrassment," Chen says.

💬 "Would I blush to say this at a dinner party?"

The logic is simple: a proper pitch is full of credentials, achievements, and reasons why you and your team are uniquely qualified — and that's simply not how people talk at dinner parties. Yet this is exactly the tone you need when speaking to investors, partners, customers, and potential employees.

credentials 🔊n. pl.
Qualifications, experience, or proof that someone is suitable for a role.
ES: credenciales
"HR verifies every finalist's credentials before making an offer."
to the exclusion of 🔊idiom
While completely leaving out or ignoring something else.
ES: excluyendo por completo a
"He focused on the coding test to the exclusion of the system-design questions."
downplay 🔊v.
To make something seem less important or less serious than it really is.
ES: minimizar, restar importancia a
"Never downplay a near-miss just to keep the safety report short."

📊 Back It Up with Evidence

There's a second mistake entrepreneurs commonly make, Chen points out: they shy away from supporting their claims with evidence. If, in a past job, you worked on an app used by millions of people — or if your current company just reached its first 10,000 users — those numbers are worth remembering and sharing. "Any traction and any validation is incredibly helpful in proving your case," Chen says.

traction 🔊n.
Visible progress or momentum toward a goal.
ES: impulso, avance
"The new onboarding process gained traction fast — three teams adopted it in a month."
validation 🔊n.
Confirmation or proof that something is correct, approved, or effective.
ES: validación
"User validation of the beta arrived faster than the team expected."

🎯 Reading the Room

This "dinner party test" is a useful reminder for any ambitious professional, not just startup founders. It points to a simple truth: what counts as socially acceptable self-confidence changes enormously depending on the situation. If you don't adjust your tone for different contexts, you're likely leaving opportunities on the table.

As Michelle Obama has pointed out, effective communication isn't really about being authentic. It's about strategy — knowing your audience, understanding what they expect, and figuring out how to tailor your message to what will actually persuade them, rather than simply saying whatever feels most "true" to you.

At an actual dinner party, you're naturally trying to fit in and make other guests comfortable. You want to come across as relatable, funny, charming, and understated. Droning on endlessly about your own achievements — and backing them up with data — works against all of that, and will likely make you look like a braggart. You'll gain nothing, and your dinner companions may start looking for the nearest exit.

But in situations where you are explicitly selling yourself or your ideas — job interviews, investor pitches, meetings with potential partners — the standards flip completely. Humility, while an extremely valuable trait in general, is neither expected nor particularly useful there. That's exactly why the "dinner party test" works so well: it's not just a helpful guide for entrepreneurs raising money — it's a useful all-purpose reminder to match your level of self-confidence to your audience.

understated 🔊adj.
Not trying to attract attention or impress people; modest in style.
ES: discreto, sobrio
"She gave an understated summary of the incident, without exaggerating the risks."
drone on 🔊phr. v.
To talk for a long time in a boring, monotonous way.
ES: hablar sin parar de forma aburrida
"He droned on about quarterly metrics until half the room stopped listening."
braggart 🔊n.
Someone who talks proudly and excessively about their own achievements.
ES: fanfarrón
"Nobody wants to pair-program with a braggart who takes credit for the team's work."
tailor 🔊v.
To adjust or design something to fit a specific need or audience.
ES: adaptar, ajustar a medida
"Tailor the safety briefing to each site — a warehouse and an office need different messaging."

💬 Discussion Questions

  • 1

    Think of someone at work whom you'd describe as genuinely confident — not cocky. What do they actually do differently from someone who comes across as arrogant?

  • 2

    Chen argues that in high-stakes professional situations — investor pitches, job interviews — you need a level of self-promotion that would feel completely out of place at a dinner party. Do you think professional contexts should really be exempt from the usual social norms around humility? Why or why not?

  • 3

    Michelle Obama's view, as quoted in the article, is that effective communication is about strategy, not authenticity — knowing your audience and tailoring your message to what will persuade them, rather than simply expressing your "true self." Do you agree, or do you think strategic communication and authenticity can coexist?

✍️ Self-Practice

1. Describe a time you undersold yourself at work. What held you back from speaking up more confidently?
There's no single correct answer — think about the specific situation, what you said (or didn't say), and what you might do differently next time.
2. Describe a time you saw someone come across as a "braggart." What exactly did they do that crossed the line?
Consider tone, timing, and audience — the same words can land very differently depending on the context.
3. Apply the "dinner party test" to a recent email, presentation, or self-introduction of yours. Would you have blushed saying it out loud at a party?
If yes, that may actually be a good sign for a professional context — remember the article's argument about matching confidence to the situation.
4. In your own field (EHS, HR, or IT), what's an example of something that would seem "cocky" to mention at a social gathering, but is essential to state clearly in a formal meeting?
Think about certifications, audit results, incident-free records, project outcomes, or technical achievements.
