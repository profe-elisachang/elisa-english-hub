Why You Like Minimalist Packaging

Researchers have discovered why shoppers prefer simple packaging designs

🎁 From Bold to Bare: A Shift in Packaging

For decades, brands fought for shoppers' attention with loud, snazzy packaging — bright colors, cartoons, and busy illustrations. The old conventional wisdom was simple: with thousands of competing products crammed onto the shelves of big-box stores and supermarkets, a product had to shout to be noticed.

Lately, though, the trend has reversed. More and more packaging looks stripped-down and uncluttered — a style that echoes the minimalist art movement of the 1960s, which stripped away anything considered unnecessary in favor of pure abstraction. Marketing researchers Rosanna K. Smith and Julio Sevilla set out to investigate what's really driving this shift — and whether shoppers genuinely prefer it.

🔬 The Research: Testing What Shoppers Will Pay

The researchers first wanted to know whether shoppers would actually pay more for products in simple packaging. To test this, they analyzed data on more than 1,000 everyday products — things like shampoo, deodorant, crackers, and cereal — sold by the largest supermarket chain in the U.S. Two research assistants independently rated how simple or complex each package design was, and the researchers averaged those ratings into a single "simplicity score."

The result: products with simpler packaging tended to have higher retail prices than similar products with busier designs. In other words, shoppers appear willing to fork over more money for a pared-down look.

To understand why, the team then recruited university students and showed them a range of packaged products, asking them to estimate a fair price, guess how many ingredients each product likely contained, and rate how "pure" the product seemed. The pattern that emerged: simplicity sent a subtle but powerful signal of purity. Simple packaging made people assume — often without solid evidence — that the product inside contained fewer preservatives, added colors, or artificial flavors.

🏷️ Brands That Got It Right

Several well-known brands illustrate how minimalist design can attract shoppers. Kashi's cereal boxes use a muted color palette and avoid cramming the box with claims or lengthy descriptions. Siggi's yogurt leans on generous white space, soft colors, and simple imagery that highlights only the most important product details.

Native deodorant and body wash take a similar approach with clear, see-through packaging and typography that gets straight to the point. Haircare brands like Ouai and Hims follow the same formula — muted tones and simple type that succinctly communicates what the product does, without visual noise.

🧴 The Psychology of "No Additives"

Shoppers' underlying desires help explain the appeal of simple design. People will pay a premium for food, cleaning supplies, or soap that they believe contains no additives or chemicals — and this may explain why participants in the study were willing to spend more on products in simple packages, regardless of whether those products actually contained fewer ingredients or not.

The desire for healthier options isn't the whole story, though. When shoppers want an indulgent treat — junk food, for example — they actually lean toward products with more complex packaging, which signals a longer ingredient list and, to them, more flavor.

⚠️ When Less Is Less

Minimalist packaging isn't a guaranteed win, though. The researchers found that products from generic brands — products without a name brand, typically sold at a lower price than their branded equivalents — don't reap the same price premium from simple packaging that name brands do.

For these products, simplicity seems to reinforce a different belief: that the brand hasn't invested much in the product's quality. In other words, the same "less" that reads as purity on a name-brand shampoo may read as cheapness on a generic one — so the simplicity of the packaging can end up signaling low investment rather than fewer chemicals or additives.

The takeaway: when it comes to minimalist design, less can often be more — but in some cases, less really is just less.
