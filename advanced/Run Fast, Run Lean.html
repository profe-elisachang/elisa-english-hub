📊 Article

📊 Run Fast, Run Lean
A former president of Tesla on how to shift your business into high gear · Source: Fast Company (adapted and paraphrased)

Jon McNeill shares five lessons from his new book, The Algorithm. He is a serial entrepreneur and a former president of Tesla. His main idea is simple: what holds back growth is often not what you are missing, but everything you have piled on.

🔑 Vocabulary

① Question every single requirement.

Companies rarely slow down because people are lazy. They slow down because of invisible rules that once made sense, but that nobody remembers creating. These rules quietly become handcuffs. Treat each requirement as guilty until proven innocent: Is it a law? Is it physics? Or is it just how we've always done things? At Tesla, foreign carmakers could not fully own a business in China. After sustained negotiations over 14 months, Tesla got the go-ahead for 100% ownership.

🔑 Vocabulary

② Delete every possible step in the process.

Speed rarely comes from adding things; it comes from subtraction. Buying a Tesla online once took 64 clicks, so Elon Musk set a stretch goal of ten. The team removed steps customers did not value, then attacked the biggest sinkhole: financing paperwork. They asked which documents the law truly required and which were simply inherited from banks. The result was a one-click loan.

🔑 Vocabulary

③ Simplify, simplify, simplify.

If a process is hard to explain, it is too complicated. If you can't explain it, you can't repeat it reliably, and if you can't repeat it, you can't scale it. The author's example is Alinea, a three-star restaurant in Chicago. Guests see something magical, but the kitchen is engineered for simplicity: tools are placed to cut out unnecessary movement, and plating steps take only seconds.

🔑 Vocabulary

④ If you can move faster than everyone else, you will win more often than you should.

This does not mean cutting corners. It means making decisions, testing and improving in short cycles, so you catch problems while they are small. In late 2021, Lululemon won the contract to outfit Team Canada for the Beijing Olympics, but had only about four months. The team had to compress its timeline. First, leaders loosened the rulebook and made decisions in real time. Second, work moved in parallel instead of one step at a time, like a busy kitchen at dinner service.

🔑 Vocabulary

⑤ Automate last.

Automation is powerful, but only once the system works. Too many companies automate a messy process, which only locks in the mess. Great startups start by hand: early Amazon did a lot manually, and DoorDash began by taking orders and delivering food by hand before writing software. You cannot automate what you do not understand.

🗣️ Discussion Questions

1. Think of a rule at your company that nobody can explain. Is it a law, a safety requirement, or just a habit? What would happen if you removed it?
2. Where do you see the biggest “sinkhole” in your department: approvals, paperwork, or tools? How would you cut it down without creating risk?
3. The author says to “fix first, then automate.” Do you agree? Describe a time when automation made a messy process worse.

✏️ Self-Practice Questions

1. What is the difference between “cutting corners” and moving fast?
Cutting corners means skipping necessary quality or safety. Moving fast means short cycles of deciding, testing and improving, so problems are caught early.
2. Complete: “Speed rarely comes from addition; it comes from ______.”
Subtraction: deleting steps that customers or the law do not require.
3. Why did Tesla's online purchase process drop from 64 clicks to a one-click loan step?
The team deleted steps customers did not value, then questioned which financing documents were truly required by law versus inherited habit.
4. Why should you “automate last”?
Automating a messy process locks the mess in. First do the work by hand, understand it, fix it, and only then automate.
