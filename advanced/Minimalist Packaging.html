🎨 Why You Like Minimalist Packaging

When less is more

Reading adaptation

For decades, marketers assumed that consumer products needed to stand out. Bright colors, bold text, cartoons, and illustrations were common ways to attract attention in crowded stores. The basic idea was simple: if thousands of products are competing for attention, companies should make their packaging as noticeable as possible.

More recently, however, there has been a move toward simplicity. Minimalist packaging removes visual elements that may seem unnecessary. Instead of filling a package with information, brands may use empty space, restrained colors, simple images, and straightforward typography. This style can communicate a very different message: the product does not need to shout for attention.

Researchers wanted to know whether shoppers actually preferred this approach—and whether they would pay more for it. They examined more than 1,000 consumer products and compared products with simpler and more complex packaging. They found that products with simpler packaging often had higher retail prices than similar products with more elaborate designs. That suggests that simplicity may influence not only what consumers like, but also what they are willing to spend.

Why would simple packaging make people pay more? One explanation is that design sends a subtle but powerful signal about the product. Consumers may associate a clean, uncluttered package with purity. They may assume it contains fewer preservatives, fewer artificial flavors, or fewer unnecessary ingredients. Shoppers are therefore not simply reacting to the package itself. They are using the package to make assumptions about what is inside.

Branding matters, too. A well-known premium brand can use simple packaging and make it look intentional. The simplicity becomes part of the brand identity. It can suggest confidence: the product does not need excessive decoration to prove its value. In this context, consumers may interpret pared-down design as evidence of quality rather than a lack of effort.

When less is less

Reading adaptation

But the effect is not universal. For generic or lesser-known brands, simple packaging can send the opposite signal. Shoppers may interpret the lack of visual investment as evidence that the company also invested less in the product. If consumers already expect a premium brand to be high quality, simplicity may reinforce that belief. If they have no such expectation, simplicity may create uncertainty.

Consumer expectations can change the meaning of the same design. When shoppers are looking for healthier options, simplicity may suggest purity and encourage them to indulge in a product they normally avoid. But when shoppers choose food associated with richer flavors or stronger ingredients, a more complex package may feel more appropriate.

So the broader lesson is not simply that “less is more.” Minimalist design works when consumers already have a reason to interpret simplicity positively. The same visual choice can communicate quality, purity, confidence, low cost, or even poor quality depending on the brand and the customer's expectations. In marketing, the design itself may be simple—but the psychology behind that design is much more complicated.

🗣️ Discussion Questions

1. When does simplicity signal quality—and when might it signal low quality?
2. Think about a product or brand you know well. What assumptions does its packaging encourage customers to make?
3. The article says the same design can send different signals depending on the brand. Do you agree? What other factors could change how customers interpret a design?
4. Can you apply this idea to your workplace? Can a simple presentation, dashboard, email, website, or report signal confidence—or can it look incomplete?

🧠 Self-Practice

1. Give the article's main argument in 30–45 seconds.
Explain that minimalist packaging can increase perceived quality, but the effect depends on the brand, product category, and consumer expectations.
2. Complete: “The interesting thing about minimalist design is that…”
Explain the difference between the physical design and the psychological meaning consumers attach to it.
3. Challenge the research: “The study may not prove that minimalist packaging causes people to pay more because…”
Consider alternative explanations such as brand reputation, product category, price positioning, or existing consumer expectations.
4. Transfer the idea to business communication.
Give one workplace situation where “less is more” could improve communication, and one where too little information could reduce credibility.
