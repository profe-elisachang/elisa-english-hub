📊 The Path of Innovation: Gateway Products

Why today’s breakthrough may be tomorrow’s bridge · by Rita McGrath

Remember the Flip video recorder? In 2009, it was a sensation—a pocket-size recorder that let ordinary people capture moments without lugging around a camcorder. Cisco acquired Flip’s maker for $590 million in stock. Two years later, Cisco shut Flip down entirely.

The Flip wasn’t a failure. It solved a real problem elegantly. But it was what I call a “gateway product”—an innovation that reveals what customers want but gets supplanted by something that delivers the same outcome more cheaply or conveniently. In this case, the rise of smartphones made a dedicated device obsolete.

The history of innovation suggests that most game changers proceed through a series of gateways. We had fax machines before email, PalmPilots before BlackBerry before iPhones, MapQuest before Google Maps. Each one made money. And each one was eventually swept aside.

camcorder
videocámara portátil
Spanish: videocámara
“She used a camcorder to film the ceremony.”
supplanted
reemplazado / desplazado
Spanish: suplantado
“Email supplanted fax for most communication.”
dedicated device
dispositivo exclusivo para una función
Spanish: dispositivo dedicado
“A GPS is a dedicated device for navigation.”

Problem solved? Gateway products genuinely solve customer problems. That’s what makes them successful—and dangerous. Their success validates the desire of customers to achieve given outcomes while obscuring the fact that the method may be temporary.

The fax machine eliminated the delay of postal mail. But it still required paper, a phone line and a compatible machine on the receiving end. Email didn’t just do the fax’s job faster—it eliminated the infrastructure entirely.

scaffolding
andamiaje / estructura de soporte
Spanish: andamiaje
“The old system was scaffolding for the new.”
form factor
factor de forma / diseño físico
Spanish: factor de forma
“The form factor of a device affects its portability.”
yet
todavía (en contexto de “aún no”)
Spanish: todavía
“Nothing else can do the job yet.”

This doesn’t mean dedicated devices always lose. Sometimes they win on performance or experience—professional cameras, high-end gaming consoles, studio monitors. But the burden is on the device to justify its separate existence. If a product’s primary advantage is that nothing else can do the job yet, leadership needs to plan for “yet” becoming “now.”

Gateway products often develop loyal following among people who’ve invested time in learning them. BlackBerry devotees became virtuosos of the physical keyboard. Customers have sunk costs, and they’re reluctant to switch. But mastery-based loyalty evaporates the moment a competitor makes it unnecessary. Smartphones didn’t require users to learn a new input language; they just worked.

virtuosos
personas con gran habilidad / maestros
Spanish: virtuosos
“He is a virtuoso of the physical keyboard.”
churn
rotación de clientes / abandono
Spanish: rotación
“High churn indicates customer dissatisfaction.”
relentlessly
incesantemente / sin descanso
Spanish: implacablemente
“Monitor your gap relentlessly.”

If your customer retention depends on what people have learned rather than what they love, you’re more vulnerable than your churn numbers suggest.

New product? Questions to ask: 1. What complexities does our product require that customers would prefer to eliminate entirely? Every negative is an opening for a competitor who does away with it. 2. If someone started fresh today with current technology, would they build this same way? If the answer is no—if they’d build something that makes your product unnecessary—you have a gateway.

3. What temporary technological gap are we exploiting? Flip cameras existed because smartphone cameras weren’t good enough yet. GPS units existed because phones lacked sensors and software. Identify your gap, and monitor it relentlessly. 4. What’s our plan for when the gap closes? This is the question most leaders avoid. Acknowledging that your hit product has an expiration date feels like disloyalty. But the alternative is being caught flat-footed.

caught flat-footed
ser tomado por sorpresa
Spanish: desprevenido
“They were caught flat-footed by the new technology.”
lured
atraído / seducido
Spanish: atraído
“Leaders are lured into thinking it’s permanent.”
tuition
lección / enseñanza (coste de aprendizaje)
Spanish: enseñanza
“Gateway products are like paying tuition.”

Is a gateway valuable? None of this means gateway products are bad businesses.

The strategic error is being lured into believing that it will be a permanent franchise. That can lead, in turn, to overinvesting in extending the product’s life, building organizations for a form factor that’s becoming obsolete and missing the chance to be the company that makes its own product unnecessary. Apple famously undermined its own hugely profitable iPod to launch the modern smartphone revolution, leading to enormous value creation.

Gateway products can be supremely valuable. They are like paying tuition to learn about the future.

📝 Self-Practice (click to reveal answers)
1. What is a “gateway product” in your own words?
A product that solves a real problem but is eventually replaced by a better, cheaper, or more convenient solution. It opens the door to customer needs but is not the final form.
2. Why does the author say “mastery-based loyalty evaporates”?
Because if customers only stay because they’ve invested time learning a product, they will leave as soon as something easier or better appears—no emotional attachment.
3. What are the four questions leaders should ask about new products?
1. What complexities can we eliminate? 2. Would we build it the same way today? 3. What temporary gap are we exploiting? 4. What’s our plan when the gap closes?
4. How can a gateway product still be “supremely valuable”?
It generates revenue, teaches companies about future markets, and can fund the next innovation—like paying tuition to learn about what comes next.
