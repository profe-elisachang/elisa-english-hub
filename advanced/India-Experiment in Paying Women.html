📰 Article

🔗 BBC News Original Article Reference

Across India, 118 million adult women in 12 states now receive unconditional cash transfers from their governments — making India the site of one of the world's largest social-policy experiments. Long accustomed to subsidising grain, fuel and rural jobs, India has stumbled into something more radical: paying adult women simply because they keep households running, bear the burden of unpaid care and form an electorate too large to ignore.

What sets India apart from Mexico, Brazil or Indonesia — countries with large conditional cash-transfer schemes — is the absence of conditions: the money arrives whether or not a child attends school or a household falls below the poverty line. The transfers range from 1,000–2,500 rupees a month — meagre sums, worth roughly 5–12% of household income, but regular. Women typically spend the money on children's education, groceries, medical expenses, retiring small debts and occasional personal items.

The political power of cash transfers was on stark display in recent elections. In Bihar, the government transferred money to 7.5 million female bank accounts just before polling. Women voted in larger numbers than men, decisively shaping the outcome. Critics called it blatant vote-buying, but the result was clear. In some policy circles, the schemes are derided as freebies. They also put pressure on state finances: 12 states are set to spend around $18bn this year, and half face revenue deficits.

Evidence is still thin but instructive. Surveys show that most women operated their own bank accounts and decided how to spend the money — mostly on food, education and medical costs. Women spoke of peace of mind, reduced marital conflict and newfound confidence. Importantly, none of the surveys finds evidence that the money discourages women from seeking paid work or entrench gender roles. The transfers strengthen financial autonomy and modestly improve bargaining power. They are neither panacea nor poison: useful but limited tools operating in a patriarchal society where cash alone cannot undo structural inequities.

💬 Discussion Questions

  • In your workplace, do you think unpaid or unrecognized work exists? How could it be acknowledged?
  • Do you think government cash transfers to women are a good policy, or do they risk becoming a political tool? Why?
  • If you were in charge of designing this kind of program for Mexico, what conditions — if any — would you add?
  • Discuss the risk of "Unconditional" contracts: In what scenario would you pay a supplier without specific KPIs? Does it build trust or encourage laziness?
  • "Administratively Simple" systems often hide complex back-ends. Discuss the challenges of scaling a database to handle 300 million active users.
  • The "Lopsided Burden" (mental load) often leads to fatigue. How can we identify workers who are carrying too much stress from home before an accident happens?

✏️ Self-Practice

  1. Fill in the blank: The new safety policy puts ________ on all department managers to report incidents immediately.
    (Hint: a two-word phrase meaning "creates pressure for")
  2. True or False: According to the article, the cash transfers caused women to stop looking for paid jobs.
  3. Choose the correct word: The small budget was (meagre / panacea / blatant), but the team made the most of it.
  4. In your own words: What does it mean when a policy is described as "neither a panacea nor poison"?
Reference Answers
  1. pressure on — "puts pressure on all department managers"
  2. False — The surveys found no evidence that the transfers discouraged women from seeking paid work.
  3. meagre — meaning very small or insufficient.
  4. It means the policy is not a perfect solution that fixes everything (panacea), but it is also not harmful or damaging (poison). It is useful but limited.
