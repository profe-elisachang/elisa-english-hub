📰 Article
⚙️ The Evolution of the Modern Workweek
— Neal Mitchell

Nowadays, many people take a five-day workweek and regular rest time for granted, but these labor conditions didn't appear overnight. They're the result of long struggles in industrial societies and a gradual shift in how work is understood. As economies developed, both working hours and attitudes toward labor changed in response to economic, social, and political forces.

During the early stages of industrialization, factory workers often worked 12 to 14 hours a day. Employers focused heavily on productivity, while workers had little power to influence their schedules. Consequently, exhaustion and unsafe working conditions were widespread. Reformers and labor activists began to speak out about whether economic growth should come at such a high human cost. This idea challenged the belief that long hours were unavoidable and gradually gained support, especially as workers began to organize and bargain. Over time, labor movements came to represent demands not only for better pay but also dignity and fairness in the workplace.

A major turning point occurred in the early 20th century, particularly during World War I. In many countries, the war created severe labor shortages as large numbers of workers were sent to the battlefield, giving those who remained greater bargaining power. Governments, worried that strikes might interrupt the production of essential supplies, stepped in to mediate labor disputes. Shorter working hours, once strongly resisted, became more widely accepted during this period. Some employers attempted to bring back longer working hours after the war ended, but workers fought these efforts. Large-scale strikes across different industries showed that attitudes toward work had permanently changed. Prolonged workdays were no longer seen as normal. Instead, reasonable working hours were increasingly viewed as necessary for a stable society.

In recent decades, this evolution has continued. Flexible schedules, remote work, and even experiments with a four-day workweek reflect a growing emphasis on work-life balance. Today, work is no longer defined solely as a mere means of survival but as just one part of a meaningful and sustainable life.

✍️ Self-Practice Questions

Click on each question to reveal a model answer.

1 What does it mean to "take something for granted"? Give your own example from daily life. +
"Take for granted" means you don't appreciate something because you assume it will always be there. For example, I take electricity for granted — I only notice it when the power goes out. At work, I sometimes take having a fixed lunch break for granted, but not all workers around the world have that.
2 Describe a situation where a manager or company "stepped in" to solve a problem at work. +
Last year, there was a conflict between our production team and the logistics department about delivery deadlines. Our supervisor stepped in and organized a meeting where both teams could explain their perspectives. After that, we set up a new communication system so the same dispute wouldn't happen again.
3 Do you think remote work or flexible schedules are sustainable for your type of job? Explain why. +
In my role in quality control, I think partial flexibility is sustainable — for example, I could do reports and data analysis from home. However, inspections and checking physical products require me to be on-site. A hybrid model might work well: 2–3 days in the factory, and the rest working remotely.
4 Use "prolonged" in a sentence about your own experience with stress or long work periods. +
During the peak season in our factory, we often work overtime for weeks at a time. After a prolonged period of 10-hour days, I notice that my concentration drops and I start making more mistakes. It reminds me that rest isn't a luxury — it's necessary to maintain quality work.
