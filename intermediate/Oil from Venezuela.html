Oil from Venezuela — Good News for Prices?

A simplified article about oil, refineries, and what it means for people in Mexico and the US.

💡   Yellow words = key vocabulary. Hover to see the meaning. Vocabulary appears after each paragraph.
Section 1
Why does this topic matter to you?
The price of fuel affects everyone. When diesel is expensive, trucks cost more to operate. This means the products you make or buy also cost more. Right now, something big is happening in global oil markets — and Mexico is directly connected. Understanding this story can help you talk about energy, costs, and trade in English at work.
Section 2
Read the simplified article
1

A large ship called the Minerva Gloria is docked in the state of Mississippi, in the southern United States. The ship is carrying 400,000 barrels of crude oil from Venezuela. Six months ago, this was impossible. Now it is happening.

crude oil

Oil that comes directly from the ground — not yet cleaned or made into fuel.

petróleo crudo

"The ship was carrying 400,000 barrels of crude oil."

2

Venezuela has the largest oil reserves in the world. But for many years, the US had sanctions against Venezuela. This means American companies could not buy Venezuelan oil. Then, in January, the US military arrested Venezuela's leader. After that, the sanctions changed, and oil started flowing again.

reserves

Oil or other resources still in the ground that can be used in the future.

reservas

"Venezuela has the largest oil reserves in the world."

sanctions

Official rules from a government that stop trade or business with another country.

sanciones

"Because of sanctions, US companies could not buy Venezuelan oil."

3

In March, Venezuela exported more than one million barrels of oil per day. This was the first time since September. The oil goes to the United States, where companies process it in refineries.

refinery

A large factory that takes crude oil and converts it into gasoline, diesel, and other fuels.

refinería

"Chevron's refinery in Mississippi processes heavy Venezuelan oil."

4

One of those companies is Chevron. Chevron has a large refinery in Pascagoula, Mississippi. It is the only major American company that can extract oil inside Venezuela. This is very useful — Chevron can take the oil, bring it to the US, and process it all by itself.

5

Venezuelan crude oil is a special type of oil. It is very heavy, thick, and dark. It has a lot of sulfur, so people call it "sour oil." This makes it harder to process. But because it is difficult, it is also cheaper to buy. The Chevron refinery was built specifically to handle this type of oil. So for Chevron, there is a strong incentive to use it.

incentive

A good reason to do something — usually because you get a benefit or reward.

incentivo

"Lower prices give companies an incentive to buy more."

6

Right now, Chevron imports about 250,000 barrels of Venezuelan oil every day. Their goal is to increase this by 50% — to around 350,000–400,000 barrels per day. Venezuela's monthly exports already surpassed one million barrels. Other companies also buy Venezuelan oil, not just Chevron.

surpassed

Went beyond a previous level or record — more than before.

superó / sobrepasó

"Venezuela's exports surpassed one million barrels per day."

7

There is another important reason why this matters right now. Iran has blocked the Strait of Hormuz — a very important shipping route in the Middle East. A lot of the world's oil normally passes through this route. Because it is blocked, global energy prices are rising. Venezuelan oil helps fill that gap.

8

More oil supply should translate to lower gasoline prices for American drivers. The US says it does not need Middle Eastern oil, so the blocked strait is not a big problem for them. But at a gas station near the Chevron refinery in Mississippi, prices are still going up.

translate to

To result in something. To have a real, practical effect.

traducirse en / resultar en

"More supply should translate to lower prices at the pump."

9

David McQueen is a retired veteran. His only income is social security — government support for older people. He says higher fuel prices are hurting his livelihood. "The price has got to go down," he says, "because I'm going down with it."

livelihood

The income and basic needs a person depends on to survive — their source of money.

sustento / medio de vida

"Higher fuel costs are hurting his livelihood."

10

A woman named Donna is also at the gas station. She only puts $30 of fuel in her car. She drives less now and spends less money on other things. She used to visit her grandchildren regularly, but it is now too expensive to drive.

11

The average gas price in Mississippi is still lower than the national average in the US. But before the conflict with Iran, gas was almost $1 cheaper per gallon. For ordinary people, that difference is significant.

Section 3
Check your understanding
1
Find the fact

Why was Venezuelan oil impossible to import six months ago? Find the sentence in the article that answers this.

Reference answer: Six months ago, the US had sanctions against Venezuela. This meant American companies were not allowed to buy Venezuelan oil. The sanctions changed after the US military arrested Venezuela's leader in January. Without that political change, the ship with 400,000 barrels would never have been allowed to dock.
2
Make the connection

The article says Venezuelan crude oil is cheap to buy, but hard to process. How is that different from the oil Mexico's PEMEX produces? What does this mean for competition?

Reference answer: PEMEX also produces heavy crude oil — similar to Venezuela's. So both countries compete for the same US refinery customers. If Venezuela sends more oil to the US, it becomes harder for Mexico to sell its oil at a good price. However, for Mexican manufacturers (not oil producers), more supply in North America could mean cheaper diesel and lower transportation costs. So the impact is different depending on whether you are a seller or a buyer of oil.
3
Think about your own life

David and Donna are changing their behavior because of fuel prices. Have you or your family done anything different because of higher energy or fuel costs? Share one example.

Example answer (this is a personal question — your answer will be different!):

"Yes. My wife and I now plan all our errands for one day instead of driving every day. This saves us money on gasoline. We also use the air conditioning less at home to reduce our electricity bill."
Section 4
Let's talk about it
🔍

Layer 1 — What does the article say?

Build confidence first
Q1 How many barrels of Venezuelan oil does Chevron import every day? What is their goal for the future?
Reference answer: Chevron currently imports about 250,000 barrels per day. Their goal is to increase this by 50%, reaching around 350,000–400,000 barrels per day. This would make Venezuelan oil one of their most important sources of supply.
Q2 Why is Venezuelan crude oil cheaper than other types of oil?
Reference answer: Venezuelan crude is cheaper to buy because it is harder to process. It is very heavy, thick, dark, and contains a lot of sulfur — that is why it is called "sour oil." Most refineries cannot handle it. Only special refineries — like Chevron's in Mississippi — are designed for this type of oil. Because fewer buyers can use it, the price is lower.
🔗

Layer 2 — Connect to Mexico

Use the article + your knowledge
Q3 Iran has blocked the Strait of Hormuz. The US says this is not a big problem for them. But could it affect energy prices in Mexico? Why or why not?
Reference answer: Yes — indirectly. Mexico does not import much Middle Eastern oil directly. But when a major shipping route is blocked, global oil prices rise for everyone. This affects the price of all oil, including what Mexico buys and sells. Higher global prices mean higher fuel costs for factories, trucks, and logistics companies in Mexico. So even a conflict far away — like Iran blocking a shipping route — affects manufacturing costs in Guanajuato or Monterrey.
Q4 More Venezuelan oil means more fuel supply in North America. How could this help or hurt manufacturers in Mexico — for example, in transportation costs?
Reference answer: More Venezuelan oil increases total supply in North America. When supply goes up, prices usually go down. This could help Mexican manufacturers by making diesel and gasoline cheaper. Lower fuel costs reduce transportation expenses — for example, moving car parts from Silao to the border, or receiving raw materials from suppliers. Even a small drop in diesel price adds up significantly over a full year across an entire supply chain.
💡

Layer 3 — Your opinion

Use your professional experience
Q5 At your company, when fuel or energy prices go up — where do you feel it first? Give a specific example if you can.
Example answer (personal — your answer will be different!):

"In my experience, we feel it first in transportation. Our logistics provider adds a fuel surcharge when diesel goes above a certain price. This makes every shipment more expensive. We also feel it in our electricity bill, because the plant uses a lot of energy to run the machines."
Q6 The article says more oil supply should translate to lower prices. But at the gas station, prices are still rising. In your experience, why does this happen?
Reference answer: There are several reasons why prices do not go down immediately, even when supply increases:

1. Time lag — It takes weeks or months for new oil to reach refineries, be processed, and arrive at gas stations.

2. Taxes and costs — The pump price includes much more than crude oil: taxes, refining, transport, and retailer margins. These do not go down automatically.

3. Global uncertainty — When there is a conflict (like with Iran), companies and traders worry about the future. This fear keeps prices high even if today's supply is enough.

4. Market speculation — Oil prices are traded on financial markets. Traders' expectations about the future affect today's price, not just current supply.
