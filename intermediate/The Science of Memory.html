📖 Article

Forgetting someone's name or where you put your keys can be annoying, but these lapses are also totally natural. We all forget things, and according to American neurologist Scott A. Small, forgetting is actually crucial for brain function. We are exposed to so much information every day that we couldn't possibly retain it all. By forgetting certain details, our brains filter out information we don't need while prioritizing information we do need. That way, we can focus on what really matters.

The systematic study of human memory was pioneered by the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus. Between 1880 and 1885, Ebbinghaus carried out studies on himself to see how quickly he forgot information. He memorized meaningless three-letter words and tested how well he could recall them after different amounts of time had passed. He found that he would forget around 50 percent of the words within an hour and 70 percent within a day. After a week, only around 25 percent of the words would still be retained.

Not only does Ebbinghaus's work show that we forget most information over time, but it also shows that we lose most new information quite soon after we first learn it. Clearly, our brains are very good at getting rid of information we don't need.

Since Ebbinghaus, other researchers have set out to understand memory—not just in humans, but in animals as well. Bonobos, for instance, have been found to have strong memories for faces over long periods. In one case, a bonobo recognized her sister and nephew even after being separated from them for 26 years.

Elephants can recall past interactions with other elephants, which helps them negotiate complicated social situations within their herds. They can also remember directions—one elephant was found to find its way back to a water source after 12 years away.

Elephants' exceptional memories largely come from their huge brains. Yet much smaller animals can have great memories too. In one study, researchers wore scary masks and trapped seven crows living around a university campus. After releasing the birds, the researchers intermittently put the masks back on. They discovered that the campus crows reacted aggressively toward them for 17 years. Interestingly, many more crows than just the original seven were hostile, suggesting that the original crows had passed their knowledge on to others.

Ultimately, for both humans and animals, remembering and forgetting are crucial survival skills. So, if you can't remember a celebrity's name or some other trivial fact, don't worry—your brain's just busy retaining much more important information.
