🌱 Trauma-Informed Leadership

Why feelings matter at work

Simplified for Intermediate Level (A2–B1)  |  Adapted from "Trauma-Informed Leadership" by Mathangi Swaminathan, Fast Company (2025)

💬 Useful Expressions
Are you okay?
¿Estás bien?
The supervisor stopped and asked, "Are you okay?" when she saw the new worker looked upset.
Take your time.
Tómate tu tiempo.
"Take your time," the trainer said to the operator learning the new machine.
I understand.
Entiendo.
When the technician explained why the report was late, the manager said, "I understand."
Do you need help?
¿Necesitas ayuda?
She noticed her teammate falling behind and asked, "Do you need help?"
Let's talk about it.
Hablemos de eso.
Instead of ignoring the problem, the supervisor said, "Let's talk about it."
📖 Article & Vocabulary

Good managers know that feelings matter at work. When workers feel safe, they do better work. This is called emotional safety. It means people can share their feelings without fear.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 1

emotional safety

feeling safe enough to share your true feelings at work

seguridad emocional

The new team leader worked hard to create emotional safety on the production line.

Ana worked at a factory. Her boss yelled at her almost every day. Ana felt very stressed. She was afraid to make mistakes. She started to feel sick before every shift.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 2

yelled

shouted in an angry voice

gritó

The supervisor yelled at the team when the shipment was late.
stressed

feeling worried and tired because of pressure

estresado/a

Many workers feel stressed during the busiest week of the month.

This story shows why managers need "trauma literacy." Trauma literacy means you notice when someone is struggling. You do not need to know their whole story. You just need to be kind and pay attention.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 3

notice

to see or become aware of something

notar, darse cuenta

A good supervisor will notice when an operator seems tired or upset.
struggling

having a difficult time with something

teniendo dificultades

The new hire was struggling to keep up with the speed of the line.

When people feel stressed, they often react in one of four ways: fight, flight, freeze, or fawn. Fight means getting angry or arguing. Flight means avoiding the problem. Freeze means feeling stuck and unable to think. Fawn means trying too hard to please other people.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 4

fight, flight, freeze, fawn

four common ways people react to stress

cuatro reacciones comunes al estrés

During the safety audit, some workers showed fight, and others showed freeze.
avoiding

trying not to deal with something

evitando

He kept avoiding the conversation about his mistake on the assembly line.

Studies show that workers who feel safe are more engaged. They stay longer at their jobs. They also recommend the company to their friends. Emotional safety is good for the whole team.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 5

engaged

interested and motivated at work

comprometido/a, motivado/a

Engaged employees are more likely to suggest ideas for improving the production line.

The first practice is simple: ask real questions. Instead of "How are you?" try asking "How are you, really?" This small change can make a difference.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 6

make a difference

to have an important, positive effect

tener un impacto, marcar una diferencia

A five-minute check-in before the shift can really make a difference.

The second practice is: don't try to fix everything. If someone shares a problem, just listen. Say, "I hear you." You do not need to solve every problem right away.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 7

fix

to solve or repair a problem

arreglar, solucionar

The technician tried to fix the machine before the next shift started.

The third practice is: ask simple questions. You can ask, "What part is difficult?" or "When did you start feeling stuck?" These questions help people think without feeling judged.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 8

stuck

unable to move forward or think clearly

atascado/a, estancado/a

She felt stuck when the new software gave an error she didn't understand.
judged

criticized or evaluated in a negative way

juzgado/a

New employees shouldn't feel judged when they ask questions about the process.

The fourth practice is: check in often. At the end of a hard day, ask your team how they feel. This helps you notice problems early and adjust the workload before people get too tired.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 9

check in

to ask someone how they are doing

preguntar cómo está alguien

The team leader checks in with each operator at the start of the shift.
workload

the amount of work a person has to do

carga de trabajo

The supervisor reduced her workload after the busy holiday season.

Trauma-informed leadership is not difficult. It just means paying attention, being patient, and building trust. When workers trust their managers, everyone works better together.

📚 Vocabulary - Paragraph 10

trust

a strong belief that someone is honest and reliable

confianza

It takes time to build trust between new employees and their supervisors.

🗣️ Discussion Questions
  • Have you ever worked with a stressed boss? How did it make you feel?
  • Which stress reaction do you think you use most: fight, flight, freeze, or fawn?
  • What can a good manager do to help workers feel safe?
✏️ Self-Practice
1. Fill in the blank: When Ana's boss shouted at her every day, she felt very _______ (adj).
2. True or False: To help someone, a manager must know a worker's whole personal history.
3. Short answer: Name the four common stress reactions from the article.
4. Workplace connection: Think of a time you felt "stuck" at work. What did (or could) your supervisor say to help?
Reference Answers
  1. stressed
  2. False — you don't need to know their whole story, just pay attention and be kind.
  3. Fight, flight, freeze, and fawn.
  4. Open-ended — answers will vary based on personal workplace experience.
