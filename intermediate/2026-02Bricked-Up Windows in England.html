🏛️ What's Up with All Those Bricked-Up Windows in England?

Visitors walking down the streets of London and other major English cities might notice a curious sight. Many of the old structures have some of their windows boarded up. What's even stranger is that there doesn't appear to be any consistent pattern as to which windows get covered.

This architectural feature dates back over 300 years during a time when "coin clipping" was a common crime in England. Back then, silver coins had simple designs with smooth, unmarked edges. Without any pattern extending to the coins' rims, it was difficult to tell if the edges had been shaved off. This made it easy for criminals to clip thin pieces of silver from around the edges of coins.

Since the coins' value came from the weight of silver they contained, a clipped coin was worth less than a full one. This became a real financial problem when taxes were paid with these lighter coins. The government ended up receiving less revenue than expected. To make up for the loss, the government introduced a property tax based on the number of windows in a building.

However, the policy led to unintended consequences. Many property owners passed the cost on to the tenants through higher rents. To avoid paying even more, many landlords began sealing windows or building houses with fewer of them. As a result, working-class families, who could only afford to live in small, shared apartments, suffered from a lack of fresh air and sunlight. Many fell ill, and diseases spread quickly through these buildings.

✍️ Self-Practice Questions

+ Why did the English government create the window tax?
The government created the window tax to make up for the revenue they lost because of "coin clipping." Criminals were shaving off pieces of silver from coins, making them lighter and worth less. When people paid taxes with these clipped coins, the government received less money than expected. To compensate for this financial loss, they introduced a property tax based on the number of windows in a building.
+ What were the unintended consequences of the window tax?
The window tax had several negative consequences that the government didn't expect. Property owners passed the cost on to tenants through higher rents. Many landlords began sealing up windows or building houses with fewer windows to avoid paying more tax. This meant that working-class families, who lived in small apartments, suffered from a lack of fresh air and sunlight. As a result, many people fell ill and diseases spread quickly through these buildings.
+ Can you think of a modern example where a well-intended policy led to unexpected problems?
Example answer (you can create your own!):
In some cities, rent control policies were created to help people afford housing. However, this sometimes led landlords to stop maintaining their properties or stop building new rental units because they couldn't make enough profit. As a result, the housing quality decreased and there were fewer apartments available, which made the problem worse for some people looking for housing.
+ Practice using the phrasal verb "end up": Describe what happened when you didn't plan something carefully at work.
Example answer (create your own!):
"Last month, we didn't plan our inventory carefully. We ended up running out of important materials and had to stop production for two days. Now we always check our stock levels at the end of each week to make sure we don't end up in that situation again."
