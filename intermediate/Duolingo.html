📱 Article

🦉 Good, Free, Fun: The Simple Formula That Made Duolingo a Daily Habit for Millions
By Natalia Guerrero · BBC Worklife · October 4, 2024

Perhaps no app has mastered user loyalty quite like Duolingo, the gamified language-learning platform that 34 million people a day can't put down. When journalist Natalia Guerrero started researching this story, Duolingo seemed to be everywhere: a friend was celebrating her 800-day Spanish practice streak, a journalist had become addicted to learning Italian, and a Sri Lankan waitress in New York crediting Duolingo for her new language skills.

📖 Vocabulary — Paragraph 1
mastered
to become very skilled at something
🇪🇸 dominar / perfeccionar
"She has mastered customer negotiations."
gamified
designed with game-like elements (points, levels, rewards)
🇪🇸 con mecánicas de juego
"Our training program is gamified to boost engagement."
streak
a series of consecutive days doing something
🇪🇸 racha
"I have a 45-day inspection streak — I check quality every day!"
addicted
unable to stop doing something (here: used informally)
🇪🇸 adicto/a / enganchado/a
"I'm addicted to checking the shipping tracker."

Duolingo's CEO and co-founder, Luis von Ahn, was born in Guatemala, a country where over half the population lives in poverty. He told the BBC: "If you have money, you can buy a very good education, but if you don't have money, sometimes you don't even learn how to read and write. That made a big impression on me." So when Von Ahn and his co-founder Severin Hacker launched Duolingo in 2012, their goal was clear: keep the app free so that education could reach everybody.

🇪🇸 lanzar
"We launched a new supplier portal last month."
poverty

🇪🇸 pobreza
"Access to education helps reduce poverty."
impression
a strong effect on how you think or feel
🇪🇸 impresión / impacto
"The factory visit made a big impression on our team."

To make money while staying free, Duolingo uses a hybrid business model: most users see ads and pay nothing, while a small group pays for a subscription that removes ads and adds extra features. Amazingly, only about 8% of users pay, yet they contribute to 80% of the company's revenue. Growth happens mainly through word of mouth — including a famous mention by Bill Gates on Reddit in 2015. By 2024, Duolingo's market capitalisation had reached $12.27 billion.

"Most of our new clients find us by word of mouth."

"Spotify and LinkedIn also use a freemium model."

So what keeps people coming back? Von Ahn says the answer is gamification and storytelling — especially the mascot, Duo the green owl, whose insistent personality inspired millions of internet memes. A streak system — where missing one day resets your count to zero — creates powerful loss aversion. The company also uses trial and error to discover which features work: "The ones that are there now worked; many others didn't."

🇪🇸 mascota (de marca)
"Our factory has a mascot on all safety posters."
loss aversion
the fear of losing something you already have — stronger than the joy of gaining something new
🇪🇸 aversión a la pérdida
"Loss aversion explains why people panic-sell stocks."
trial and error

"We improved the process through trial and error."

When the BBC asked about the challenges of running a large company, Von Ahn was surprisingly open. He explained that as the company grew from 50 to 850 employees, it became harder to build trust with everyone personally. But his biggest personal challenge is something many of us recognize: he avoids conflict. "I am a people-pleaser," he admitted. "Sometimes I have to tell people something they don't like — and that's hard for me." He linked this to his Latin American upbringing: in Guatemala, people tend to go around the subject rather than say difficult things directly — a style that can cause misunderstandings in international business. His Swedish wife, by contrast, is extremely direct: "Swedish people just say: 'I don't like you.' And it's like — wait, why are you saying that?"

This indirect communication style once caused Von Ahn a very awkward situation. When he needed to fire his very first employee — a woman at his small startup — he was so indirect that she never realized she had been dismissed. She came back to work the next day, thinking they had simply had a good conversation. He had to fire her a second time, this time clearly. The lesson he learned: when delivering difficult news, say the hardest words first. "You have to start by saying: 'I am so sorry, I'm going to have to let you go' — say it first, so it's super clear."

Looking ahead, Von Ahn has an ambitious vision for Duolingo. The app already teaches 41 languages, plus maths and music. He believes that in time, people will be able to learn almost anything through an app. However, he does not think apps will replace schools or universities, because those institutions serve other important purposes — schools provide childcare, and universities teach young people how to be adults. As for growth markets, Duolingo is focusing on countries like the US, UK, Japan, India, Brazil, and Spanish-speaking Latin America — a region that, for Von Ahn, has always been close to his heart.

✏️ Self-Practice Questions

1. Duolingo is free for most users. How does the company make money? ＋

Sample answer: Duolingo uses a freemium model. About 90% of users see ads and pay nothing. Around 8% pay for a premium subscription with extra features like no ads. Even though most users are free, that small group of subscribers brings in about 80% of the company's revenue. The rest comes from ads.

2. What is a "streak" and why does Duolingo use it? ＋

Sample answer: A streak is a count of consecutive days you use the app. If you miss one day, it resets to zero. Duolingo uses streaks to create a daily habit — people don't want to lose their progress, so they open the app every day. This is called loss aversion: we hate losing something more than we enjoy gaining something new.

3. Why did Von Ahn describe himself as a "people-pleaser"? How did this cause a problem at work? ＋

Sample answer: Von Ahn said he hates conflict and always wants to make people happy. This made it very difficult to deliver bad news directly. When he tried to fire his first employee, he was so indirect that she never realized she was dismissed — she came back to work the next day! He learned he needed to say the difficult message first and clearly: "I'm sorry, I'm going to have to let you go."

4. Does Von Ahn think apps like Duolingo will replace schools and universities? Why or why not? ＋

Sample answer: No, he does not think apps will fully replace schools or universities. He believes you can learn almost any subject through an app, but schools and universities serve other important purposes. Schools provide childcare for families, and universities help young people learn how to be adults and navigate life. An app can teach content, but it cannot replace those social and developmental experiences.

5. In your own words, what is Duolingo's "simple formula" for success? ＋

Sample answer: The formula is: Good + Free + Fun. The app must actually teach well, it must be free so that anyone can access it, and it must feel like a game — not a boring class. They also use characters with personality, a streak system, social media presence, and continuous trial and error to build a brand that people love and recommend to their friends.
